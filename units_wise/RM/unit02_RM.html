Topic 2.1

Definition, Purpose, and Importance of Research Design

Research Design is the overall plan or blueprint for conducting a research study. It provides the framework that guides the collection, measurement, and analysis of data. A research design ensures that the evidence obtained effectively addresses the research problem logically and as unambiguously as possible.
📌 Last Night Tip
Research Design answers the question: HOW will this research be conducted? It is the master plan specifying methods for data collection and analysis. A good design minimizes bias and maximizes the reliability of evidence.

Definitions of Research Design

  • Kerlinger (1986): "Research design is the plan, structure and strategy of investigation conceived so as to obtain answers to research questions and to control variance."
  • Kothari: "Research design is the arrangement of conditions for collection and analysis of data in a manner that aims to combine relevance to the research purpose with economy in procedure."
  • Zikmund: "Research design is a master plan specifying the methods and procedures for collecting and analyzing the needed information."

Purpose of Research Design

🎯

Provide Direction

Offers a clear roadmap for conducting the study, ensuring all activities are aligned with research objectives.

🔬

Ensure Validity

Helps obtain valid, objective, and accurate answers to research questions by controlling extraneous variables.

⏱️

Guide Resource Allocation

Optimizes the use of time, money, and effort by focusing on essential activities.

📊

Facilitate Analysis

Ensures collected data is appropriate for planned statistical analysis techniques.

🔁

Enable Replication

Provides sufficient detail for other researchers to replicate the study, verifying findings.

⚠️

Minimize Bias

Includes controls to reduce systematic errors and researcher bias.

Fig 2.1 — The Role of Research Design in the Research Process

Importance of a Well-Structured Research Design

✓ Why a Good Design Matters

  • Reduces Ambiguity: Clearly defines what data to collect, from whom, and how
  • Increases Confidence: Well-designed studies produce trustworthy results
  • Saves Resources: Prevents wasted effort on irrelevant data collection
  • Enables Causal Inference: Only strong designs can establish cause-effect relationships
  • Facilitates Peer Review: Clear design helps reviewers evaluate study quality
  • Supports Generalization: Proper sampling design enables population inference
  • Enhances Replicability: Detailed design allows other researchers to replicate

✗ Consequences of Poor Design

  • Confounded Results: Cannot determine which variable caused observed effects
  • Wasted Resources: Time and money spent on unusable data
  • Biased Conclusions: Systematic errors lead to wrong answers
  • Low Power: Cannot detect real effects even when they exist
  • Non-replicable: Insufficient detail for others to repeat study
  • Questionable Validity: Results cannot be trusted for decision-making
Poor Design vs Good Design — Example

Research Question: "Does a new teaching method improve student learning outcomes in a programming course?"

POOR DESIGN (What NOT to do):

  • Use one class taught with new method, compare to general population averages
  • No pre-test to measure prior knowledge
  • Different instructors for control and treatment groups
  • Different exam difficulty for post-test
  • Problem: Cannot tell if improvement is due to teaching method, instructor skill, or easier exam

GOOD DESIGN (What TO do):

  • Randomly assign students to control (traditional) and treatment (new method) groups
  • Same instructor teaches both groups
  • Pre-test and post-test with same exam difficulty
  • Control for student prior knowledge statistically
  • Result: Can confidently attribute differences to teaching method
References
  • Kerlinger, F.N. — Foundations of Behavioral Research, Holt, Rinehart and Winston
  • Kothari, C.R. — Research Methodology: Methods and Techniques, New Age International
  • Creswell, J.W. — Research Design: Qualitative, Quantitative, and Mixed Methods Approaches
Topic 2.2

Types of Research Designs: Exploratory, Descriptive, Experimental, Quasi-Experimental

Research designs can be classified based on the purpose of the study and the degree of control the researcher has over variables. The four main types are Exploratory Design, Descriptive Design, Experimental Design, and Quasi-Experimental Design.
📌 Exam Tip
Remember the hierarchy: Exploratory (loose) → Descriptive (structured) → Quasi-experimental (partial control) → Experimental (full control). As control increases, ability to infer causation increases, but external validity may decrease.
Fig 2.2 — Spectrum of Research Designs (Control vs Realism)

1. Exploratory Research Design

🔍 Exploratory Design — Characteristics

Definition: Exploratory design is used when the research problem is not clearly defined. It helps researchers gain initial insights, develop hypotheses, and identify key variables for future research.

Purpose: To explore new or under-researched areas; to formulate precise research questions; to understand the nature of a problem.

When to use:

  • Little is known about the topic
  • The problem is not well understood
  • Need to develop hypotheses for testing
  • Want to identify key variables and relationships
  • Studying a new or emerging phenomenon

Methods used: Literature review, expert interviews, focus groups, case studies, pilot studies.

Example: "Exploring the challenges faced by remote software development teams" — conducting 15 in-depth interviews with team leads to identify key issues before designing a larger survey.

📋 Exploratory Design — Key Features

  • Flexibility: Highly flexible and unstructured approach
  • No hypothesis: Typically does not test pre-formulated hypotheses
  • Small sample: Usually involves small, purposive samples
  • Qualitative focus: Primarily qualitative methods
  • Not generalizable: Findings are not statistically generalizable
  • Generative: Produces hypotheses rather than testing them

Advantages: Cost-effective, provides direction, identifies real issues

Limitations: Findings not conclusive, potential for researcher bias

2. Descriptive Research Design

📝 Descriptive Design — Characteristics

Definition: Descriptive design aims to accurately and systematically describe a population, situation, or phenomenon. It answers "what," "where," "when," and "how" questions.

Purpose: To describe characteristics of a population; to estimate proportions; to document facts and features.

When to use:

  • The research problem is well-defined
  • Need to describe population characteristics
  • Want to estimate prevalence or frequency
  • Conducting surveys or observational studies
  • Need baseline data for decision-making

Methods used: Surveys (cross-sectional or longitudinal), observational studies, secondary data analysis.

Example: "A descriptive study of smartphone usage patterns among college students in Pune" — survey of 500 students about daily usage, apps, and time spent.

📊 Descriptive Design — Key Features

  • Pre-planned: Structured methodology with clear protocols
  • Large sample: Large, representative samples for generalization
  • Quantitative focus: Often uses quantitative methods
  • No manipulation: Researcher does not manipulate variables
  • No causation: Describes but does not explain causes
  • Cross-sectional or longitudinal: Single or multiple time points

Types of Descriptive Studies:

  • Cross-sectional: Data collected at one point in time
  • Longitudinal: Data collected at multiple time points
  • Case study: In-depth study of a single case
  • Comparative: Comparing two or more groups
Descriptive Research Example — Software Development Practices Survey

Research Question: "What agile practices are commonly used in Indian IT companies?"

Design: Cross-sectional survey of 200 software professionals

Variables measured:

  • Company size, industry domain, years agile experience
  • Practices used (Scrum, Kanban, XP, etc.)
  • Frequency of ceremonies (daily stand-ups, retrospectives)
  • Challenges faced in adoption

Findings: 78% use Scrum, 45% use daily stand-ups, most common challenge is "changing requirements" (62%)

3. Experimental Research Design

🧪 Experimental Design — Characteristics

Definition: Experimental design is a rigorous design where the researcher manipulates one or more independent variables, controls extraneous variables, and measures the effect on dependent variables to establish cause-effect relationships.

Purpose: To establish causal relationships; to test hypotheses about cause and effect.

When to use:

  • Need to establish causation (not just correlation)
  • Can manipulate the independent variable
  • Can randomly assign participants to conditions
  • Need high internal validity
  • Testing cause-effect hypotheses

Methods used: Laboratory experiments, field experiments (RCTs).

📐 Experimental Design — Key Features

  • Random assignment: Participants randomly assigned to groups
  • Manipulation: Researcher controls independent variable
  • Control group: Group that does not receive treatment
  • Pretest/Posttest: Measures before and after manipulation
  • Control of confounds: Extraneous variables controlled
  • High internal validity: Strong causal inference

Classic Experimental Designs:

  • Posttest-only control group: R X O
  • Pretest-posttest control group: R O X O
  • Solomon four-group: Combines both with additional controls
Fig 2.3 — Classic Experimental Design: Pretest-Posttest Control Group Design
Experimental Design Example — Testing a New Code Review Tool

Research Question: "Does using a new automated code review tool reduce the number of bugs in software?"

Design: Pretest-Posttest Control Group Design

Participants: 60 software developers randomly assigned to:

  • Control Group (n=30): Traditional manual code review
  • Treatment Group (n=30): New automated tool + manual review

Procedure:

  1. Pretest: All developers review same code sample; measure bugs found
  2. Treatment: Control group continues manual; treatment group uses new tool for 2 weeks
  3. Posttest: All review another equivalent code sample; measure bugs found

Analysis: Compare improvement (posttest-pretest) between groups using t-test

Finding: If treatment group shows significantly greater improvement, the tool is effective

4. Quasi-Experimental Design

⚖️ Quasi-Experimental Design — Characteristics

Definition: Quasi-experimental design resembles experimental design but lacks random assignment of participants to groups. It is used when random assignment is not feasible or ethical.

Purpose: To establish causal relationships when true experiments are not possible; to evaluate interventions in real-world settings.

When to use:

  • Random assignment is not feasible (e.g., existing classrooms, organizations)
  • Ethical constraints prevent withholding treatment
  • Working with natural or pre-existing groups
  • Real-world program evaluation

Methods used: Non-equivalent control group design, interrupted time series, regression discontinuity.

📐 Quasi-Experimental — Key Types

1. Non-equivalent Control Group Design:

  • Treatment and control groups but NO random assignment
  • Pretest and posttest for both groups
  • Statistical adjustment for pre-existing differences

2. Interrupted Time Series:

  • Multiple measurements before and after intervention
  • No control group; uses trend analysis
  • Can detect if change is due to intervention or natural trend

3. Regression Discontinuity Design:

  • Assignment based on cutoff score
  • Those above cutoff get treatment, below do not
  • Compares outcomes near cutoff
Quasi-Experimental Example — Evaluating a Training Program

Research Question: "Does a mandatory agile training program improve team productivity in an IT company?"

Design: Non-equivalent Control Group Design

Context:

  • Treatment Group (Team A, n=8): Attends mandatory 2-day agile training (company policy)
  • Control Group (Team B, n=10): No training (different department, similar work)
  • Why not random: Cannot randomly assign employees; training is mandatory for Team A

Measure: Story points completed per sprint (4 weeks before training, 4 weeks after)

Analysis: Compare improvement between groups using ANCOVA (controlling for baseline productivity)

Caution: Groups may differ in ways other than training (team composition, project type, management style)

Topic 2.3

Components of Research Design: Objectives, Hypotheses, Variables, Methods, Sampling Design

A comprehensive research design consists of several interconnected components that together form a complete blueprint for the study. These components ensure that all aspects of the research are coherent and aligned with the research problem.
📌 Last Night Tip
Remember the 5 key components: Objectives, Hypotheses, Variables, Methods, Sampling. Each component must align with the others for a valid design.
Fig 2.4 — Components of Research Design

1. Research Objectives

Definition: Research objectives are clear, specific statements of what the research intends to achieve. They translate the research problem into actionable goals.

Writing Good Research Objectives — SMART Criteria

SMART Objectives are:

  • Specific — Clearly defined, not vague
  • Measurable — Can be quantified or assessed
  • Achievable — Realistic given resources
  • Relevant — Aligned with research problem
  • Time-bound — Achievable within timeframe

Example Research Problem: "Low adoption of automated testing in small software companies"

Poor Objective: "To study automated testing adoption"

Good Objectives (SMART):

  1. To determine the current adoption rate of automated testing among software companies in Pune with ≤50 employees
  2. To identify the top three barriers to automated testing adoption in small companies
  3. To evaluate the relationship between team size and automated testing adoption

2. Hypotheses

Definition: A hypothesis is a tentative statement or educated guess about the relationship between two or more variables. It is a proposed explanation that can be tested through research.

📝 Types of Hypotheses

  • Null Hypothesis (H₀): States no relationship or no difference. What the researcher tries to disprove.
  • Alternative Hypothesis (H₁ or Hₐ): States there is a relationship or difference. What the researcher wants to support.
  • Directional Hypothesis: Specifies the direction of the relationship (e.g., "greater than," "less than").
  • Non-directional Hypothesis: States a relationship exists but not the direction.
  • Research Hypothesis: Same as alternative hypothesis.
  • Statistical Hypothesis: Formulated for statistical testing.

📐 Hypothesis Examples

Research Question: "Does code review frequency affect software quality?"

  • Null (H₀): There is no significant relationship between code review frequency and defect density.
  • Alternative (H₁): There is a significant relationship between code review frequency and defect density.
  • Directional (H₁): Higher code review frequency is associated with lower defect density.

Characteristics of Good Hypotheses:

  • Testable (can be verified empirically)
  • Falsifiable (can be proven wrong)
  • Specific and clear
  • Logically related to theory
  • States expected relationship

3. Variables

Definition: A variable is any characteristic, attribute, or quantity that can vary or take on different values. Variables are the "things" that researchers measure, manipulate, or control.

Fig 2.5 — Types of Variables and Their Relationships

Types of Variables — Detailed Explanation

Variable TypeDefinitionExample (Code Review Study)
Independent Variable (IV)Variable manipulated or changed by researcher; the presumed causeCode review frequency (Daily vs Weekly vs None)
Dependent Variable (DV)Variable measured to see effect of IV; the presumed effectDefect density (bugs per thousand lines of code)
Moderating VariableAffects the strength/direction of IV-DV relationshipDeveloper experience (effect of reviews may be stronger for junior developers)
Mediating VariableExplains the process through which IV affects DVCode understanding (reviews improve understanding, which then reduces defects)
Control VariableHeld constant to prevent influence on resultsProject type, programming language, team size
Confounding VariableExtraneous variable correlated with both IV and DV; threatens validityTeam motivation (motivated teams may do more reviews AND produce better code)
Variable Identification — E-commerce Research Example

Research Question: "Does website loading time affect customer purchase intention?"

  • Independent Variable (IV): Website loading time (2 sec, 5 sec, 10 sec)
  • Dependent Variable (DV): Purchase intention (measured on 1-7 scale)
  • Control Variables: Product type, price, website design, customer demographics
  • Moderator: Customer patience level (may weaken/strengthen the effect)
  • Confounding Variable: Internet speed of customer (slower internet = longer loading time AND less likely to purchase)

4. Methods of Data Collection

📋

Surveys & Questionnaires

Standardized questions to collect self-reported data from samples. Efficient for large samples. Can be online, paper, or interview-administered.

When: Measuring attitudes, opinions, behaviors, demographics

🔬

Experiments

Controlled manipulation of variables to establish causation. Requires random assignment and control groups.

When: Testing cause-effect hypotheses

👥

Interviews

Direct questioning of participants. Can be structured (fixed questions) or unstructured (exploratory).

When: In-depth understanding, sensitive topics

👁️

Observation

Systematically watching and recording behavior. Can be participant (researcher involved) or non-participant.

When: Studying actual behavior, not self-reports

📁

Secondary Data

Using existing data collected by others (government statistics, company records, previous research).

When: Cost-effective, longitudinal analysis

💻

System/Software Metrics

For CS research: logs, performance data, code repositories, bug tracking systems.

When: Software engineering research, performance studies

5. Sampling Design

Definition: Sampling design specifies how participants or units will be selected from the population. It defines the sampling frame, sampling technique, and sample size.

Fig 2.6 — Types of Sampling Designs

Probability Sampling (Random Selection)

  • Simple Random Sampling: Every member has equal chance; use random number generator
  • Systematic Sampling: Select every kth member from list (k = population size / sample size)
  • Stratified Sampling: Divide population into strata (groups), random sample from each stratum
  • Cluster Sampling: Divide into clusters, randomly select clusters, sample all members in selected clusters

Non-Probability Sampling (Non-Random Selection)

  • Convenience Sampling: Select readily available participants
  • Purposive (Judgmental) Sampling: Researcher selects participants based on specific criteria
  • Snowball Sampling: Existing participants recruit future participants (for hard-to-reach populations)
  • Quota Sampling: Pre-specified quotas for subgroups (like stratified but non-random)
Sampling Design Example — Software Developer Survey

Population: All software developers in India (estimated 5 million)

Sampling Frame: Members of LinkedIn "Software Developers India" group (200,000 members)

Sample Size Needed: 384 (for 95% confidence, 5% margin of error)

Sampling Method Selected: Stratified random sampling by:

  • Stratum 1: Experience level (Junior, Mid, Senior)
  • Stratum 2: Company type (Product, Service, Startup)
  • Randomly select from each stratum proportional to population

Justification: Ensures representation across experience levels and company types; allows subgroup analysis

Topic 2.4

Validity and Reliability: Internal Validity, External Validity, Construct Validity, Reliability and Consistency

Validity refers to whether a study measures what it claims to measure (accuracy). Reliability refers to whether a study produces consistent results if repeated (precision). Both are essential for trustworthy research — you need reliability to have validity, but reliability alone does not guarantee validity.
📌 Last Night Tip
Remember the analogy: A reliable scale always gives the same weight (consistent). A valid scale gives the TRUE weight (accurate). A scale could be reliable but not valid (always gives 5kg for a 10kg object). In research, you need BOTH.
Fig 2.7 — Validity vs Reliability: Target Analogy

Internal Validity

Definition: Internal validity is the extent to which a study establishes a trustworthy cause-and-effect relationship between the independent and dependent variables. It asks: "Can we be confident that the independent variable caused the observed change, not something else?"

Threats to Internal Validity

ThreatDescriptionExample
HistoryExternal events occurring between pretest and posttestCompany-wide training during study affects productivity, not just the treatment
MaturationNatural changes in participants over timeDevelopers become more skilled naturally, not due to new tool
TestingTaking pretest affects posttest scoresPretest makes participants aware of issues, changing posttest behavior
InstrumentationChanges in measurement instrument or observersDifferent defect detection tool used for pretest vs posttest
Statistical RegressionExtreme scores tend to move toward meanVery low-performing team improves regardless of treatment
SelectionPre-existing differences between groupsTreatment group had more senior developers from the start
Mortality/AttritionDropout of participants from studyDissatisfied developers left treatment group, biasing results
Selection-InteractionSelection interacts with other threatsOnly motivated developers volunteered for treatment group

Ways to Improve Internal Validity

  • Random assignment to groups (best protection)
  • Control group to compare against treatment
  • Pretest-posttest design to measure change
  • Blinding (participants and/or researchers unaware of group assignment)
  • Standardized procedures for all groups
  • Statistical controls (ANCOVA) for pre-existing differences

External Validity

Definition: External validity is the extent to which study findings can be generalized to other populations, settings, times, and contexts. It asks: "Can these results be applied beyond this specific study?"

Types of Generalization

  • Population Generalization: To other people (different demographics, locations)
  • Setting Generalization: To other contexts (different organizations, environments)
  • Temporal Generalization: To other times (different years, technology generations)
  • Ecological Generalization: To real-world settings (from laboratory to field)

Threats to External Validity

ThreatDescriptionExample
Sample CharacteristicsSample not representative of populationStudy uses only students → cannot generalize to professional developers
Setting CharacteristicsArtificial setting differs from real worldLab experiment with simple tasks → not generalizable to complex real projects
Time of StudyFindings may be time-boundStudy from 2010 on waterfall methods → not relevant for agile teams today
Reactive Effects of TestingPretest sensitizes participantsTesting makes participants more aware → not generalizable to unaware population
Interaction of Selection and TreatmentTreatment effect only works for specific type of participantNew tool only helps experienced developers, not juniors

Ways to Improve External Validity

  • Random sampling from population
  • Replication across different populations, settings, times
  • Real-world settings (field experiments rather than lab)
  • Diverse samples (different companies, experience levels, locations)
  • Multi-site studies to capture variation

Construct Validity

Definition: Construct validity is the extent to which the measurements used in a study actually represent the theoretical constructs they are supposed to measure. It asks: "Are we measuring what we think we are measuring?"

Construct Validity Example — Measuring "Developer Productivity"

Construct: "Developer Productivity" (theoretical concept)

Possible Measures (with validity concerns):

  • Lines of code written: Poor construct validity (doesn't capture quality, complexity, value)
  • Story points completed: Moderate validity (better, but varies by team estimation)
  • Features delivered meeting quality standards: Good validity (captures output and quality)
  • Combined metric: Features × Quality ÷ Time: Best (multiple indicators of same construct)

Improving Construct Validity:

  • Use multiple measures of the same construct
  • Use existing validated instruments from literature
  • Conduct pilot studies to check if measures make sense to participants
  • Convergent validity: Measure correlates with similar constructs
  • Discriminant validity: Measure does NOT correlate with different constructs

Types of Construct Validity

  • Convergent Validity: Measure correlates highly with other measures of the same construct
  • Discriminant Validity: Measure does NOT correlate with measures of different constructs
  • Face Validity: Measure appears to measure what it claims (superficial, expert judgment)
  • Content Validity: Measure covers all aspects of the construct (comprehensive)
  • Criterion Validity: Measure predicts outcomes it should predict (concurrent or predictive)

Reliability and Consistency

Definition: Reliability is the consistency or repeatability of measurements. A reliable measurement produces the same results under consistent conditions.

Fig 2.8 — Types of Reliability

Types of Reliability

TypeDescriptionHow to Measure
Test-Retest Reliability Consistency of measure over time. Same test administered twice to same participants. Correlation between Time 1 and Time 2 scores (should be high, >0.7)
Internal Consistency Consistency across items within a single test. Items measuring same construct should correlate. Cronbach's alpha (α > 0.7 acceptable, >0.8 good, >0.9 excellent)
Inter-Rater Reliability Consistency across different raters or observers. Multiple observers give similar ratings. Cohen's kappa, intraclass correlation (ICC > 0.7 acceptable)
Parallel Forms Reliability Consistency across different versions of the same test. Two equivalent forms. Correlation between Form A and Form B scores
Reliability Example — Code Quality Assessment

Context: Three code reviewers rating code quality on a scale of 1-10

Reliability Check (Inter-Rater):

File 3989

Inter-rater correlation: High (ratings are consistent across reviewers) → Good reliability

But validity question: Does a "7" actually represent good code? A reliable scale could be consistently wrong if the construct definition is poor.

Unit 2 — Key Takeaway

Research design is the blueprint that guides all aspects of a study. A well-structured design defines clear objectives, testable hypotheses, properly identified variables, appropriate data collection methods, and sound sampling strategies. The four types of research designs serve different purposes: exploratory for initial investigation, descriptive for characterization, quasi-experimental for real-world causal inference, and experimental for rigorous cause-effect testing. Finally, the validity and reliability of a study determine its trustworthiness — internal validity for causal confidence, external validity for generalizability, construct validity for measurement accuracy, and reliability for consistency. Master these concepts to design rigorous, credible research studies.
