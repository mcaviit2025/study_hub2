Topic 1.1

Introduction to Cyber Crime and Cyber Space

Cyber Space is the virtual environment created by interconnected digital systems — the internet, private networks, devices, and the data flowing through them. It is not a physical place but a domain where electronic communication, commerce, social interaction, governance, and increasingly warfare occur. Cyber Crime is any criminal or unlawful activity committed using cyberspace as the medium, the target, or both.
📌 Last Night Tip
Know three roles a computer plays in crime: Tool (phishing, fraud), Target (hacking, ransomware), Witness (log files as evidence). India's primary law is the IT Act 2000 (amended 2008). Cyberspace was first defined by William Gibson in his 1984 novel Neuromancer.

Defining Cyber Space

Cyberspace encompasses:

  • The global internet and all connected private networks (intranets, extranets)
  • All digital devices — computers, smartphones, IoT devices, servers, routers
  • All data stored, processed, or transmitted across these systems
  • The software, operating systems, and applications running on these devices
  • Human users interacting with and through these systems
Key Characteristics of Cyberspace
Borderless: A cybercriminal in Russia can attack a bank in Mumbai in milliseconds — national borders mean nothing.
Anonymous: VPNs, Tor, and fake identities make attribution extremely difficult.
Asymmetric: A single attacker with minimal resources can cause damage worth billions.
Persistent: Once data is stolen or published online, it can never be fully recalled.
Interconnected: Attacking one system can cascade through hundreds of connected systems.

Defining Cyber Crime

Cyber Crime is any illegal act for which knowledge of computer technology is essential for its perpetration, investigation, or prosecution. It can broadly be classified based on the role of the computer:

Computer RoleDefinitionExampleIT Act Section
Computer as Target The computer or network itself is the victim — the attacker seeks to damage, disrupt, or infiltrate it Hacking a bank server; ransomware on hospital systems; DDoS on government websites Sec 43, 66, 66F
Computer as Tool The computer is used to commit a traditional crime in a new digital medium Online fraud; phishing; cyberstalking; distribution of obscene material Sec 66C, 66D, 67
Computer as Witness The computer contains digital evidence of a crime (online or offline) Email logs proving insider trading; CCTV footage; WhatsApp messages as evidence IT Act Sec 65B (evidence admissibility)

Topic 1.2

History and Evolution of Cyber Crime

Cyber crime did not begin with the internet. It evolved gradually alongside computing technology — from early telephone-line manipulation in the 1960s, through the first computer viruses in the 1980s, to the sophisticated nation-state attacks and AI-assisted fraud of the 2020s. Understanding this evolution is essential to predicting where threats will emerge next.

Key Milestones in the History of Cyber Crime

1960s — The Era of Phone Phreaking
Birth of System Exploitation
Phone Phreaking — manipulating telephone switching systems using tone signals to make free long-distance calls. John Draper ("Captain Crunch") discovered that a toy whistle from a cereal box produced a 2600 Hz tone that gave free access to AT&T long-distance lines. This was the world's first recorded exploitation of a technological system. These early phreakers became the first generation of hackers.
1970s — First Computer Frauds
Insider Crimes and Early Hacking
The first documented computer crimes were financial fraud by insiders with privileged access — bank employees manipulating account records, employees stealing computer time. The first hacking tools (password crackers, network sniffers) emerged from academic and military research networks. ARPANET — the predecessor to the internet — was already being probed for weaknesses.
1980s — The Virus Era Begins
First Malware and the "414s" Case
The Morris Worm (1988) — the first major internet worm, created by Robert Morris — crashed approximately 6,000 computers (10% of the internet at that time). The 414s hacker group broke into 60 computer systems including Los Alamos National Laboratory. The first computer viruses appeared on floppy disks. Pakistan's Brain virus (1986) was the first IBM PC virus — ironically created by two brothers to protect their software from piracy. USA passes the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) 1986.
1990s — The Internet Crime Explosion
Web-Based Crime, Kevin Mitnick, and Y2K
The commercialisation of the internet in the early 1990s created an explosion of new crime opportunities. Kevin Mitnick — the world's most famous hacker — was arrested in 1995 for hacking into dozens of corporate and government systems. Credit card fraud moved online. The first web defacements occurred. Phishing attacks were coined in 1996, targeting AOL users. India passed the IT Act 2000 — its first cybercrime legislation — at the end of this era.
2000s — Organised Crime Goes Online
Botnets, Identity Theft, and Nation-State Attacks
Cybercrime professionalised. Botnets allowed criminals to control millions of infected machines. Identity theft became a mass crime — the TJX data breach (2007) exposed 94 million card records. Stuxnet (2010) — a joint US-Israel operation — physically destroyed Iranian nuclear centrifuges using a computer worm, marking the birth of cyber warfare as a geopolitical tool. India amended its IT Act in 2008 to address new threats like identity theft, cyber terrorism, and cyber stalking.
2010s — Ransomware, APTs, and Mega-Breaches
Ransomware Epidemic and Nation-State Espionage
Ransomware became a billion-dollar criminal industry — WannaCry (2017) infected 230,000 systems in 150 countries. The SolarWinds supply-chain attack (2020) compromised 18,000 organisations including US Federal agencies undetected for 9 months. Social media became a major vector for fraud, fake news, and influence operations. India saw the rise of the Jamtara vishing and SIM swap gangs. Data breaches exposed billions of records globally.
2020s — AI-Powered Crime and Deepfakes
AI-Assisted Fraud, Crypto Crime, and Digital Arrests
Artificial Intelligence dramatically lowered the skill barrier for cybercrime — AI generates convincing phishing emails, voice clones for vishing, and deepfake videos for fraud. Cryptocurrency enabled anonymous ransom payments and money laundering. In India, the "Digital Arrest" scam emerged — fake CBI/police video calls extorting crores from victims. The DPDPA 2023 and India's expanding cyber law framework respond to this new threat landscape.

Topic 1.3

Traditional Problems Associated with Computer Crime

Computer crime presents unique challenges that make it fundamentally different from traditional physical crime. These challenges exist at every level — detection, investigation, prosecution, and prevention — and stem from the technical nature of digital evidence, the borderless nature of the internet, and the rapid pace at which technology outpaces law.

Core Traditional Problems

🌍

Jurisdictional Problems

A cybercriminal in Country A attacks a victim in Country B using servers in Country C. Which country's law applies? Whose police have authority to investigate and arrest? Different countries have different laws, extradition treaties, and cooperation levels — many countries do not extradite their own citizens for computer crimes.

Example: Jamtara gang operated in India targeting Indian banks — but when attackers are overseas, extradition becomes near-impossible
🕶️

Anonymity of the Attacker

Attackers use VPNs, proxy servers, the Tor network, and fake identities to conceal their real IP address and identity. Tracing an attack through multiple international anonymisation layers — each in a different legal jurisdiction — can take months or years, by which time the attacker has moved on.

Example: A phishing server hosted in Romania, accessed through Tor, run by someone in Nigeria — each hop requires a separate legal order
💾

Digital Evidence Challenges

Digital evidence is volatile, easily altered, and requires strict handling procedures (chain of custody, hashing, Section 65B certificate in India) to be admissible in court. Metadata may be spoofed. Logs may be cleared. Encrypted communications may be mathematically impossible to read. Many courts still struggle to evaluate complex technical evidence.

Anvar P.V. vs P.K. Basheer (2015): Supreme Court ruled electronic evidence inadmissible without Section 65B certificate
⏱️

Speed of Attack vs Speed of Law

A cyber attack can be planned, executed, and the attacker can cover their tracks — all within minutes. By the time a victim notices, reports the crime, a police officer registers an FIR, and an investigating officer with the right skills takes up the case, days or weeks may have passed — and evidence is long gone.

WannaCry encrypted 230,000 systems in 24 hours — legal system takes months to investigate
📚

Lack of Awareness and Reporting

Most victims of cyber crime never report it — out of embarrassment, fear of reputational damage (corporations), or simply not knowing how. Organisations hide data breaches to avoid regulatory penalties and public loss of trust. This means official crime statistics dramatically undercount the true scale of cyber crime.

Only an estimated 1 in 10 cybercrime victims reports the crime to police (NASSCOM study)
👮

Shortage of Skilled Investigators

Investigating cyber crime requires specialised skills in digital forensics, network analysis, malware analysis, and legal procedure — skills that most police officers do not have. India's cyber crime cells are overwhelmed, understaffed, and often lack modern forensic tools. Training investigators takes years while the threat evolves by the month.

India has fewer than 2,000 certified digital forensic investigators for a country of 1.4 billion people
⚖️

Inadequate Legal Framework

Laws take years to draft, debate, and pass. Technology evolves in months. The IT Act 2000 was written before smartphones, social media, cloud computing, or AI existed — it has struggled to keep pace. New offences (deepfakes, crypto crime, AI fraud) often have no specific legal provision and must be prosecuted under general provisions that may not perfectly fit.

No specific Indian law for deepfake crimes as of 2024 — prosecuted under IT Act Sec 66E or 67A
🔁

Difficulty of Attribution

Proving in court that a specific person committed a specific cyber attack is extremely difficult. IP addresses can be spoofed. Malware can be deployed through compromised third-party machines. Defence attorneys routinely challenge attribution — "someone else used my Wi-Fi," "I was infected with a RAT," "the logs were fabricated." Technical attribution requires multiple independent strands of evidence.

Nation-state attackers specifically use tools and code from other countries' hacking groups to mislead attribution
🌐

Lack of International Cooperation

Cybercrime is inherently international but law enforcement is national. The Budapest Convention on Cybercrime (2001) — the main international treaty — has only 65 signatories and India is not among them. Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties (MLATs) for obtaining evidence from foreign countries can take 6–18 months — by which time servers are wiped and suspects have fled.

India relies on bilateral arrangements with the USA to obtain data from US tech companies — each request takes months
⚠️ The Dark Figure of Cyber Crime
Criminologists use the term "dark figure" to describe the gap between actual crime and reported crime. In cyber crime, the dark figure is enormous — estimates suggest only 5–15% of cyber crimes are ever reported. This makes accurate crime statistics nearly impossible and policy planning extremely difficult. The dark figure is caused by victim embarrassment, organisational self-interest in hiding breaches, lack of awareness of reporting channels, and low confidence that reporting will lead to any meaningful outcome.

Topic 1.4

Computer Security

Computer Security (also called Cybersecurity or Information Technology Security) is the protection of computer systems, networks, programs, and data from digital attacks, unauthorised access, damage, or disruption. Its goal is to ensure that information systems continue to work as intended, that data retains its integrity, and that only authorised parties can access, modify, or destroy information.

The CIA Triad — Foundation of Computer Security

All computer security objectives can be reduced to three core properties known as the CIA Triad. Every security control, policy, and tool serves at least one of these three goals:

C Confidentiality

Information is accessible only to those who are authorised to access it. Prevents unauthorised disclosure — keeping secrets secret. Achieved through encryption, access controls, and need-to-know policies.

Threat: A hacker stealing your email login credentials and reading your private messages violates Confidentiality.
Control: AES-256 encryption of stored data; MFA for login.
I Integrity

Information and systems are only modified by authorised parties through authorised processes. Prevents unauthorised alteration — ensuring data is accurate and trustworthy. Achieved through hashing, digital signatures, and access controls.

Threat: An attacker altering ₹50,000 to ₹5,00,000 in a bank transaction record violates Integrity.
Control: SHA-256 checksums; write-protected logs; digital signatures.
A Availability

Systems, networks, and data are accessible to authorised users when they need them. Prevents denial of service — ensuring business continuity. Achieved through redundancy, backups, DDoS protection, and disaster recovery.

Threat: A DDoS attack flooding a hospital's patient management system making it unavailable violates Availability.
Control: CDN, load balancers, redundant servers, offline backups.
📌 Extended Model — CIA + AAA
Modern security models extend CIA with three additional properties: Authentication (verifying identity — who are you?), Authorisation (verifying permissions — what can you do?), and Accountability / Non-Repudiation (ensuring actions can be traced — who did what and when?). Together these six properties form a complete security framework.

Goals of Computer Security

GoalDescriptionHow Achieved
PreventionStop attacks and unauthorised access from occurring in the first placeFirewalls, strong passwords, MFA, patch management, security awareness training
DetectionIdentify attacks and security incidents as quickly as possibleSIEM, IDS/IPS, antivirus, log monitoring, security audits
ResponseContain and eradicate threats, minimise damage when incidents occurIncident response plan, SOC team, forensic investigation, law enforcement engagement
RecoveryRestore systems and data to normal operation after an incidentBackups, disaster recovery plan, business continuity plan

Levels of Computer Security

💻

Physical Security

Protecting the physical hardware from theft, damage, or unauthorised physical access. Includes locked server rooms, security guards, CCTV, biometric access controls, and environmental protections against fire and flood.

🌐

Network Security

Protecting data as it travels across networks and protecting the network infrastructure itself. Includes firewalls, VPNs, network segmentation, IDS/IPS, Wi-Fi security (WPA3), and monitoring for unusual traffic patterns.

📱

Application Security

Protecting software applications from vulnerabilities and exploitation. Includes secure coding practices, code review, web application firewalls (WAF), input validation, regular security testing, and patch management.

🗄️

Data Security

Protecting data wherever it lives — at rest (stored on disk), in transit (moving across a network), and in use (being processed in memory). Includes encryption, access controls, data classification, and data loss prevention (DLP).

🆔

Identity & Access Management

Ensuring that only authorised users can access systems and data, and only to the extent permitted by their role. Includes authentication (passwords, MFA, biometrics), authorisation (RBAC), and identity governance.

🧑‍💼

Operational Security

The policies, procedures, and practices that ensure day-to-day security operations are effective. Includes security policies, employee training, incident response procedures, vendor management, and change management controls.

Topic 1.5

Threats, Harm, and Vulnerabilities

Three fundamental concepts underpin all of computer security: Threat (the potential danger), Vulnerability (the weakness that makes the threat possible), and Harm (the actual damage caused when a threat exploits a vulnerability). Risk exists at the intersection of all three.
Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Impact (Harm)

Understanding Threats

A Threat is any potential event or action that could cause harm to an information system or its data. Threats can be:

Intentional Threats

  • Deliberate acts by human threat actors with malicious intent
  • External: hackers, organised crime, nation-states, competitors
  • Internal: disgruntled employees, insider espionage, sabotage
  • Examples: hacking, phishing, ransomware, data theft, corporate espionage
  • These are the primary focus of cyber security defences

Unintentional Threats

  • Accidental harm caused by human error or environmental factors
  • Employee mistakes: misconfigured servers, accidental data deletion
  • Natural disasters: fire, flood, earthquake, power surge destroying hardware
  • System failures: hardware malfunctions, software bugs, network outages
  • Just as dangerous as intentional threats — often more frequent

Understanding Vulnerabilities

A Vulnerability is a weakness or gap in a system's security — in software, hardware, processes, or human behaviour — that can be exploited by a threat to cause harm.

Vulnerability TypeDescriptionExample
Software VulnerabilityBugs, flaws, or errors in code that can be exploitedUnpatched OS, SQL injection flaw in login page, Log4Shell
Hardware VulnerabilityPhysical flaws or weaknesses in hardware designSpectre/Meltdown CPU flaws; unencrypted laptop; open USB port
Network VulnerabilityWeaknesses in network configuration or protocolsOpen Wi-Fi, unencrypted Telnet, weak firewall rules, default router password
Human VulnerabilityPeople are susceptible to manipulation and make mistakesEmployee clicks phishing link; shares password; uses "123456"
Configuration WeaknessSystems configured insecurely — often during deploymentAWS S3 bucket left public; default admin credentials not changed
Procedural WeaknessInadequate security policies or failure to follow themNo patch management process; no backup policy; no access review

Understanding Harm

Harm (also called Impact) is the actual damage caused to an organisation, individual, or system when a threat successfully exploits a vulnerability. Harm can be categorised as:

💸

Financial Harm

Direct monetary losses — theft of funds, ransom payments, cost of recovery, regulatory fines, lost business, and litigation costs. Often the most immediately measurable impact.

Avg cost of a data breach in India: ₹17.9 crore (IBM Cost of a Data Breach Report 2023)
🏆

Reputational Harm

Loss of customer trust, brand damage, and decline in business value. Often more severe and long-lasting than financial losses — customers switch to competitors after a breach and may never return.

Sony PlayStation Network breach (2011): 77 million accounts; network offline 23 days; $170M in losses
⚖️

Legal / Regulatory Harm

Penalties under data protection laws (DPDPA 2023 — up to ₹250 crore), IT Act, sectoral regulations (RBI, SEBI). Directors may face personal criminal liability for negligent security practices.

GDPR fine: Meta (WhatsApp) fined €225 million for transparency violations (2022)
🧠

Psychological Harm

Suffered by individuals — victims of cyberstalking, cyber bullying, identity theft, sextortion, and romance scams often experience severe anxiety, depression, PTSD, and in extreme cases, suicidal ideation.

Sextortion victims report depression, social withdrawal, and academic failure as primary impacts
🏗️

Operational Harm

Disruption to business processes and service delivery. Ransomware that disables hospital systems can delay surgeries, divert ambulances, and directly endanger patient lives. OT/ICS attacks can disrupt power, water, or transport services.

AIIMS Delhi ransomware (2022): Hospital operations disrupted for 2+ weeks; patient data of 3–4 crore affected
🔐

Data Harm

Loss, theft, or corruption of valuable data — personal records, intellectual property, trade secrets, research data. Unlike physical theft, data can be copied — the original may remain while a thief has a perfect duplicate.

CoWIN vaccination data breach (2023): Aadhaar, phone numbers, vaccine certificates of crores of Indians reportedly leaked

Topic 1.6

Cyber Security Fundamentals

Cyber Security is the body of technologies, processes, and practices designed to protect networks, devices, programs, and data from attack, damage, or unauthorised access — with the overarching goal of maintaining the CIA triad (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) across all digital assets. It is not a product you buy — it is an ongoing process of risk management.

Core Principles of Cyber Security

🛡️

Defence in Depth

Apply multiple overlapping layers of security controls so that if one fails, others remain. Like a medieval castle — moat, walls, guards, keep. No single control is relied upon absolutely. Also called "layered security."

🔑

Least Privilege

Every user, process, and system should have the minimum level of access rights needed to perform its function — and nothing more. Limits the damage if an account is compromised. A cashier should not have access to the entire bank database.

🚫

Separation of Duties

Critical tasks should require the involvement of more than one person. No single person should be able to perform a sensitive action from start to finish. Prevents insider fraud. A fund transfer should require both initiation AND approval by different people.

🎯

Zero Trust

"Never trust, always verify." Every user, device, and network connection — even internal ones — must be continuously authenticated and authorised. Assumes breach: no network is inherently trusted. The modern replacement for perimeter-only security.

🔒

Security by Design

Security must be built into systems from the very beginning — not bolted on as an afterthought. Applies to software development (secure SDLC), network architecture (DMZ from day one), and policy development (privacy by design).

📝

Accountability

Every action on a system should be traceable to a specific user or process — through audit logs, monitoring, and digital signatures. Accountability deters insider threats and supports forensic investigation after incidents.

Cyber Security Framework — NIST CSF 2.0 (6 Functions)

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) is the most widely adopted global framework for managing cyber security risk. Version 2.0 (2024) adds a sixth function — Govern — to the original five:

GV Govern Establish cybersecurity risk management strategy, policy, roles and responsibilities (new in CSF 2.0)
ID Identify Understand assets, risks, governance, and supply chain — what needs protecting?
PR Protect Implement safeguards — access control, encryption, training, secure development
DE Detect Monitor for anomalies and security events — SIEM, IDS, log analysis, threat intelligence
RS Respond Take action on detected incidents — contain, eradicate, communicate, improve
RC Recover Restore capabilities after incidents — backups, disaster recovery, lessons learned

Topic 1.7

Types of Cybercrime

Cybercrime is not a single offence — it is a broad umbrella covering dozens of distinct criminal activities united by their use of digital technology. Classification helps law enforcement apply the correct legal provision, helps organisations prioritise their defences, and helps victims identify where to report.

Primary Classification — By Target

🖥️

Crimes Against Computer Systems

The computer or network is the primary victim. The attacker seeks to gain unauthorised access, disrupt operations, steal stored data, or destroy system integrity.

Hacking · Ransomware · DDoS · Malware · Data Breach · System Sabotage
👤

Crimes Against Individuals

Targeting specific people to harm them financially, psychologically, reputationally, or physically — using digital technology as the weapon or medium of attack.

Cyber Stalking · Cyber Bullying · Identity Theft · Sextortion · Harassment · Defamation
🏢

Crimes Against Organisations

Targeting businesses, government bodies, or institutions to steal intellectual property, financial data, classified information, or to disrupt their operations.

Corporate Espionage · Trade Secret Theft · BEC / CEO Fraud · Insider Theft · Supply Chain Attack
🏛️

Crimes Against Society / State

Threatening public order, national security, or democratic institutions. The most severe category — often involves nation-state actors or organised terrorist groups.

Cyber Terrorism · Critical Infrastructure Attack · Election Interference · Disinformation Campaign
💰

Financial / Economic Cybercrimes

A broad category of crimes motivated by financial gain — using digital technology to steal money, evade taxes, launder proceeds of crime, or commit fraud at scale.

Online Banking Fraud · UPI Fraud · Crypto Theft · Investment Scam · Phishing · Lottery Fraud
🌑

Content-Based Cybercrimes

Creation, distribution, or possession of illegal digital content — material that is harmful, prohibited, or used to facilitate other crimes.

CSAM · Hate Speech Online · Fake News · Obscene Material · Piracy · Deepfake Pornography

Specific Types of Cybercrime — Detailed

1. Hacking (Unauthorised Access)

Gaining access to a computer system or network without permission. Ranges from exploratory ("I just wanted to see if I could") to malicious (stealing data, planting malware, destroying systems). Punishable under IT Act Section 66 — up to 3 years imprisonment and ₹5 lakh fine.

2. Phishing

Sending fraudulent emails, messages, or creating fake websites impersonating legitimate organisations to trick victims into revealing credentials, financial information, or installing malware. The most prevalent cybercrime globally. Under IT Act Section 66D (cheating by impersonation using computer resource).

3. Identity Theft

Stealing personal information (name, Aadhaar, PAN, bank account details, OTP) to impersonate the victim — for financial fraud, credit card crime, or avoiding detection for other crimes. IT Act Section 66C — up to 3 years imprisonment and ₹1 lakh fine.

4. Ransomware

Malware that encrypts the victim's files and demands payment (typically in cryptocurrency) for the decryption key. Modern ransomware groups also steal data before encrypting ("double extortion") and threaten to publish it. India's AIIMS Delhi attack (2022) is the landmark Indian ransomware case.

5. Cyberstalking

Using digital technology to persistently follow, monitor, harass, or threaten a specific individual — causing them fear and distress. Covered under IT Act Section 66A (struck down in 2015), now prosecuted under IPC/BNS Section 354D. Detailed in Topic 1.9.

6. Cyber Bullying

Using digital platforms to deliberately harm, harass, humiliate, or threaten individuals — particularly common among school-age children and teenagers. Detailed in Topic 1.9.

7. Online Fraud and Scams

Includes banking fraud, investment scams, lottery fraud, job offer fraud, and the emerging "Digital Arrest" scam unique to India. Total financial fraud losses in India exceeded ₹11,000 crore in 2023 (MHA I4C data).

8. Denial of Service (DoS / DDoS)

Flooding a server or network with traffic to make it unavailable to legitimate users. Distributed DoS uses thousands of compromised machines (botnet) to amplify the attack. Targets include bank websites, government portals, and e-commerce platforms during peak periods.

9. Child Sexual Abuse Material (CSAM)

Creation, distribution, or possession of sexual material involving minors. The most severely punished cybercrime — IT Act Section 67B: up to 5 years (first offence) or 7 years (subsequent) imprisonment. Actively monitored by INTERPOL, CBI, and state cyber cells in India.

Topic 1.8

Hacking & Ethical Hacking

Hacking is the practice of identifying and exploiting weaknesses in a computer system or network — with or without authorisation. While popularly associated with criminal activity, hacking itself is a neutral technical skill. The intent and authorisation of the person performing it determine whether it is a crime or a valuable security service.

Types of Hackers — The Hat Classification

🤍

White Hat Hacker

  • Also called Ethical Hackers or Penetration Testers
  • Always have explicit written permission from the system owner before testing
  • Discover vulnerabilities and report them responsibly to the organisation
  • Work within defined scope and rules of engagement
  • Help organisations fix weaknesses before criminals can exploit them
  • Often hold certifications: CEH, OSCP, CISSP
  • Legal status: Completely legal and encouraged
🖤

Black Hat Hacker

  • Malicious hackers who attack systems without authorisation
  • Motivated by financial gain, revenge, notoriety, or ideology
  • Steal data, plant malware, extort victims, sell stolen credentials
  • Often part of organised criminal groups or nation-state units
  • Cover tracks to avoid detection and prosecution
  • Constitute the primary threat actors in cybersecurity
  • Legal status: Criminal — IT Act Section 66 and beyond
🩶

Grey Hat Hacker

  • Operate between white and black hat — break in without permission but don't cause deliberate harm
  • Typically discover a vulnerability, then inform the victim organisation (sometimes demanding payment)
  • Do not steal data or deploy malware — purely exploratory
  • May publicly disclose vulnerability if organisation ignores them
  • Controversial: well-intentioned but still operating illegally
  • Legal status: Still illegal — unauthorised access regardless of intent
Other Hacker Categories
Script Kiddie: Uses pre-written tools without understanding the underlying techniques. Low skill, high nuisance value.
Hacktivist: Ideologically or politically motivated — Anonymous, India vs Pakistan cyber skirmishes.
Nation-State Hacker: Government-sponsored — APT28 (Russia), Lazarus Group (North Korea), APT41 (China).
Insider Threat: Legitimate employee abusing trusted access.
Cyber Terrorist: Uses hacking to cause fear, harm, or advance a political/religious agenda.

Ethical Hacking — Penetration Testing

Ethical Hacking (Penetration Testing or "pen testing") is the authorised, systematic attempt to penetrate a target system's defences to find vulnerabilities before malicious hackers do. It is a critical component of any mature security programme.

The Penetration Testing Methodology — 5 Phases

1 Reconnaissance Gather intelligence about the target — OSINT, DNS lookups, WHOIS, social media. Passive (no direct contact) or Active (scanning).
2 Scanning & Enumeration Identify open ports, running services, OS versions, and potential vulnerabilities using tools like Nmap, Nessus, Nikto.
3 Gaining Access Exploit identified vulnerabilities to enter the target system — Metasploit, Burp Suite, custom exploits. Document every step.
4 Maintaining Access Simulate how an attacker would establish persistence (backdoors, scheduled tasks). Test if detection systems alert on this behaviour.
5 Reporting Document all findings with evidence, risk ratings, and specific remediation recommendations. Present to client. Remove all test artefacts.

Types of Penetration Tests

TypeTester's KnowledgeSimulatesBest For
Black Box TestNo prior knowledge of target — starts from zero like a real attackerExternal attacker with no inside informationTesting external attack surface; most realistic scenario
White Box TestFull knowledge — source code, architecture, credentials, network diagrams providedInsider threat or attacker with extensive intelligenceThorough code review; maximum coverage in minimum time
Grey Box TestPartial knowledge — some credentials, network diagrams but not full source code accessCompromised insider account or authenticated web userMost common approach — balances realism with thoroughness

Ethical Hacking vs Malicious Hacking — Key Differences

Ethical Hacking ✅

  • Written authorisation from the system owner (Rules of Engagement)
  • Defined scope — only agreed systems are tested
  • All findings reported to the organisation
  • No data stolen or used for personal gain
  • All test access removed after engagement
  • Conducted by certified professionals (CEH, OSCP)
  • Legal and encouraged by organisations and regulators

Malicious Hacking ❌

  • No authorisation — purely criminal act
  • No scope boundaries — attackers target anything of value
  • Findings used for personal gain, sold, or published maliciously
  • Data stolen, systems damaged, malware planted
  • Backdoors left for persistent future access
  • Conducted anonymously to evade law enforcement
  • Illegal — IT Act Sec 66, up to 3 years + fine
📌 Bug Bounty Programmes — India Context
Major Indian organisations run Bug Bounty Programmes — publicly inviting ethical hackers to discover and report vulnerabilities in their systems in exchange for monetary rewards. Flipkart, NPCI (UPI), Indian Government's CERT-IN, and private banks have such programmes. Globally, Google, Microsoft, Facebook, and Apple pay millions in bug bounties annually. This is the intersection of ethical hacking and commercial security practice.

Topic 1.9

Cyber Bullying and Cyber Stalking

Cyber Bullying and Cyber Stalking are two of the most psychologically damaging forms of cybercrime — primarily because they target individuals personally, are often sustained over long periods, and can follow the victim into every space in their digital life. Unlike physical bullying or stalking, the digital form never stops when the victim "goes home."

Cyber Bullying — Definition and Forms

Cyber Bullying is the use of digital technology — social media, messaging apps, online gaming platforms, email — to deliberately and repeatedly harm, intimidate, humiliate, or exclude another person. It differs from a single rude message or argument: it is systematic, sustained, and intended to cause harm.

📌 Key Distinction — Cyber Bullying vs Online Conflict
Cyber Bullying has three defining characteristics: it is intentional (deliberate, not accidental), it is repetitive (happens more than once or persists), and there is a power imbalance (the bully has some advantage — popularity, numbers, personal information — over the victim). A one-time rude comment, however hurtful, is not cyber bullying by this definition.

Forms of Cyber Bullying

HarassmentSending repeated offensive, rude, or threatening messages to a personFlooding someone's Instagram DMs with abusive messages after a disagreement
CyberstalkingPersistent monitoring, following, and threatening — overlaps significantly with cyber stalking (covered next)Tracking someone's location through their social media check-ins; monitoring all their posts

Cyber Stalking — Definition and Characteristics

Cyber Stalking is the use of electronic communication to stalk or harass a person or group — involving persistent, unwanted surveillance, contact, or threats that cause fear in the victim. It is the digital equivalent of physical stalking — following, watching, and threatening — but with the added power of technology: it can be done remotely, anonymously, and 24 hours a day.

Characteristics of Cyber Stalking

  • Persistent: Repeated contact or surveillance over an extended period — not a single incident
  • Unwanted: The victim has made clear (or it is obvious) that the contact is not welcome
  • Fear-inducing: A reasonable person in the victim's position would feel fear for their safety or wellbeing
  • Often escalates: Begins with online contact, may escalate to physical surveillance, threats, or violence
  • Can be anonymous: Stalker may hide behind fake accounts, making it difficult for victims to identify or report them

Methods Used in Cyber Stalking

📍

Location Tracking

Using social media check-ins, geotagged photos, or covertly installed spyware/stalkerware to monitor the victim's physical location in real time without their knowledge.

Extracting GPS coordinates from photos posted on Instagram to locate the victim's home
📧

Persistent Messaging

Flooding the victim with unwanted messages, calls, emails, or social media interactions — often switching to new accounts when blocked. The volume and persistence itself constitutes the threat.

Creating 50+ fake WhatsApp accounts after being blocked to continue contact
🖥️

Identity Monitoring

Systematically watching and archiving all of the victim's online activity — every post, every photo, every comment — to build a profile and maintain a sense of control over the victim's life.

Screenshotting every social media post; noting every person the victim interacts with online
🎭

False Reporting / Campaigns

Filing false reports against the victim with platforms, employers, or police. Organising "cancel campaigns" or coordinated harassment by multiple accounts — also called "brigading" or "dogpiling."

Mass-reporting a journalist's social media account as spam to get it suspended
☎️

Threatening Communication

Sending explicit threats of violence, sexual harm, or damage to reputation — sometimes targeting the victim's family members or employers to amplify the intimidation.

"I know where you live and I will come there" sent via anonymous email after identifying the victim's address
📱

Spyware / Stalkerware

Installing hidden monitoring software on the victim's phone or computer — often by an intimate partner — to read all messages, track location, listen to calls, and take covert photographs.

Commercially available "parental control" apps repurposed to monitor an intimate partner without consent

🇮🇳 Real Case — Cyber Stalking in India
In one of India's earliest landmark cyber stalking cases, Manish Kathuria created a fake Yahoo! Messenger profile impersonating Ritu Kohli, a Delhi woman, and posted her phone number in online chat rooms inviting men to call her with obscene messages at all hours. She received hundreds of threatening and obscene calls. She complained to Delhi Police, and Kathuria was arrested under the then-applicable IPC Section 509 (words/gestures intended to insult the modesty of a woman). This case directly contributed to cyber stalking being specifically addressed in the IT Act 2000 amendment of 2008.

Topic 1.10

Impact of Cyber Bullying and Cyber Stalking

The harm caused by cyber bullying and cyber stalking extends far beyond what is immediately visible. Unlike a bruise from physical assault, the wounds are psychological — and they can last for years. The digital nature of these crimes means the harm is permanent (screenshots, posts that cannot be deleted), pervasive (it follows the victim into every space — home, school, work), and witnessed by an audience (peer humiliation is amplified by social media).
⚠️ Mental Health Note
This topic covers serious mental health impacts including depression, anxiety, and in some cases suicidal ideation. If you or someone you know is experiencing distress related to online harassment, please reach out to: iCall (India): 9152987821 · Vandrevala Foundation: 1860-2662-345 · National Cyber Crime Helpline: 1930. You are not alone, and help is available.

Impact on Individuals — Victims

🧠

Psychological and Mental Health Impact

The most severe and lasting harm. Victims consistently report persistent anxiety, clinical depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), panic attacks, feelings of helplessness, and low self-worth. Many develop social anxiety disorder — fearing online interaction entirely. In the most tragic cases, severe cyber bullying has led to suicide — documented cases include teenagers in India, the USA, and the UK. The "always-on" nature of social media means there is no escape, no safe space to recover — the bullying follows the victim to bed, to the dinner table, to the bathroom.

📚

Academic and Professional Impact

Victims — particularly school and college students — experience significant decline in academic performance. Inability to concentrate, reluctance to attend school (absenteeism), dropping from courses or extracurricular activities, and in severe cases, dropping out of education entirely. Adults who are cyber stalked or harassed at work lose productivity, request transfers, take extended medical leave, or resign — with serious long-term career consequences. The constant state of hypervigilance (always checking if they're being watched or targeted) consumes enormous cognitive resources.

🤝

Social and Relationship Impact

Victims withdraw from social interactions — both online and offline — out of fear, embarrassment, and distrust. Friendships deteriorate as victims become isolated. Romantic relationships are damaged — either by the stalker's interference or by the victim's inability to trust new relationships. Family relationships suffer as victims struggle to explain their changed behaviour and as families themselves become targeted (stalkers often contact victims' family members to amplify pressure). The victim's entire social world can collapse inward over time.

💸

Financial Impact

Direct costs include therapy and counselling fees, legal fees for restraining orders or criminal complaints, costs of changing phone numbers, email addresses, and social media accounts. Reputational damage from false information spread by bullies or stalkers can cost victims employment, business contracts, and professional opportunities. Sextortion victims may pay ransoms to prevent image release — losing significant sums with no guarantee of compliance from the extortionist. Identity theft (often accompanying cyber stalking) causes direct financial losses and years of credit repair.

😴

Physical Health Impact

The chronic stress of being persistently targeted manifests physically — disrupted sleep (insomnia, nightmares), appetite changes (loss of appetite or stress-eating), headaches, stomach problems, and a weakened immune system. In extreme cases, victims develop psychosomatic conditions directly linked to their trauma. Physical safety is also a direct concern when online stalking escalates to physical surveillance, showing up at the victim's home or workplace, or explicit threats of physical violence — which are not rare in the most severe cases.

🔒

Digital Life and Privacy Impact

Victims often delete social media accounts, change phone numbers, use pseudonyms online, and avoid posting photos or location information — essentially being driven off the digital public sphere. This digital exile has its own costs: loss of social connection, professional networking opportunities, and the right to participate freely in public discourse. Some victims move homes, change jobs, or alter their daily routines to avoid a stalker — losing the freedom and spontaneity of normal life.

Impact on Families

Cyber bullying and stalking do not only affect the direct victim. Families experience:

  • Parental distress: Parents of cyber bullied children report significant anxiety, guilt ("I should have monitored their online use"), and helplessness — especially when the school or police cannot immediately intervene
  • Sibling impact: Younger siblings may be targeted as a way to reach or pressure the primary victim
  • Family conflict: Disagreements about how to respond — remove all devices? Change schools? Involve police? — create tension within families
  • Financial burden: Therapy, legal costs, and potentially relocation fall on the family unit

Societal Impact

LevelImpact
School / College LevelToxic classroom and campus culture; fear among students; reduced participation; schools forced to develop anti-cyber bullying policies; strain on counselling resources

Prevention and Response Strategies

🧑‍🏫

Education and Awareness

Age-appropriate cyber safety education in schools; digital citizenship curricula; teacher and parent training on recognising signs of cyber bullying; awareness campaigns by CERT-IN, NCW, and state police.

📱

Platform-Level Controls

Reporting tools on all social media platforms; content moderation; account suspension for harassment; blocking and privacy controls; anonymous tip reporting; platform cooperation with law enforcement.

⚖️

Legal Action and Support

File complaint on cybercrime.gov.in; call helpline 1930; approach local cyber crime police cell; seek restraining order; preserve evidence (screenshots with timestamps); engage victim support organisations (NCW, iCall).

Unit 1 — Final Takeaway

Cyber security is not merely a technical discipline — it is a social, legal, and human challenge. From understanding what cyber space is and how crime evolved within it, to grasping the psychological devastation of cyber bullying and stalking, Unit 1 establishes that security is ultimately about protecting people — their money, their data, their safety, their mental health, and their right to participate freely in the digital world. Every technical control we study in later units exists in service of this fundamentally human goal.
