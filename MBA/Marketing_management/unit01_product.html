Topic 1

Product: Meaning & Definition

A Product is anything that can be offered to a market for attention, acquisition, use, or consumption that might satisfy a want or need. It includes physical objects, services, persons, places, organizations, and ideas.

Definition of Product

According to Philip Kotler, "A product is anything that can be offered to a market to satisfy a want or need. Products that are marketed include physical goods, services, experiences, events, persons, places, properties, organizations, information, and ideas."

Real World Example
When you buy an iPhone, you are not just buying a phone. You are buying communication, status, user experience, ecosystem access, and customer service. This is the total product offering.
Exam Tip
Remember: Product = Anything that satisfies customer needs. It includes BOTH tangible (goods) and intangible (services) elements.

  • Kotler, P., & Keller, K. L. — Marketing Management, 15th Edition

Topic 2

The Role of Product as a Market Offering

A Market Offering is the total value proposition that a company presents to customers. The product is the CORE of this offering, but it also includes services, benefits, and experiences.

Why Product is Central to Market Offering

1. Satisfies Customer Needs

The product is the primary means through which a company fulfills customer requirements. Without a good product, no amount of promotion or pricing can succeed.

2. Creates Value Proposition

The product determines the value customers perceive. A strong product creates competitive advantage and brand loyalty.

3. Drives Marketing Mix (4Ps)

Product is the first P of the marketing mix. Price, Place, and Promotion decisions all depend on the product's nature and lifecycle stage.

4. Generates Revenue

Product sales are the primary source of revenue for most businesses. Without a product that customers want, there is no business.

Topic 3

Goods & Services Continuum

The Goods-Services Continuum shows that most products are not purely goods or purely services but fall somewhere in between. Pure goods have no service element, while pure services have no tangible component.
Fig 3.1 — Goods-Services Continuum
PURE GOODS
Salt, Soap, Toothpaste
PURE SERVICES
Teaching, Healthcare, Consulting

Types of Products on the Continuum

Type Description Examples
Pure Goods Tangible products with no service component Salt, Sugar, Soap, Petrol
Goods with Services Tangible products that require after-sales service Car, Refrigerator, Mobile phone
Hybrid (50-50) Equal mix of goods and services Restaurant meal, Hotel stay
Services with Goods Primarily service but some tangible elements Air travel (food, ticket), Insurance (policy document)
Pure Services Intangible, no physical product Teaching, Doctor consultation, Legal advice
Example — McDonald's
McDonald's offers a hybrid product: the burger is tangible (good), but the fast service, cleanliness, and dining experience are services. This is why it's not a pure goods company.
Key Insight

Almost every product today is a combination of goods and services. Successful companies add services to differentiate their physical products.

Topic 4

Classification of Consumer Products

Consumer products are products bought by final consumers for personal consumption. They are classified based on consumer buying behavior.

Four Types of Consumer Products

1. Convenience Products

Products that customers buy frequently, immediately, and with minimal comparison and buying effort. They are low-priced and widely available.

Examples
Soap, toothpaste, newspapers, soft drinks, snacks, milk.

2. Shopping Products

Products that consumers compare on bases such as suitability, quality, price, and style before purchasing. They are bought less frequently.

Examples
Furniture, clothing, mobile phones, laptops, televisions, cars.

3. Specialty Products

Products with unique characteristics or brand identification for which a significant group of buyers is willing to make a special purchase effort.

Examples
Luxury cars (Mercedes, BMW), designer clothes (Gucci, Louis Vuitton), Rolex watches, specific gourmet foods.

4. Unsought Products

Products that the consumer either does not know about or knows about but does not normally consider buying. They require aggressive advertising and personal selling.

Examples
Life insurance, encyclopedias, funeral services, blood donations, fire extinguishers.

Topic 5

Classification of Industrial Products

Industrial products are products bought by individuals and organizations for further processing or for use in conducting a business. They are classified into three main categories.

Three Types of Industrial Products

1. Materials and Parts

Products that enter the manufacturer's product completely. They include raw materials (farm products, natural products) and manufactured materials and parts (component parts, component materials).

Examples
Raw materials: Iron ore, crude oil, wheat, cotton.
Manufactured: Tires for cars, processors for computers, leather for shoes.

2. Capital Items

Long-lasting products that facilitate developing or managing the finished product. They include installations (factories, offices) and equipment (generators, computers, elevators).

Examples
Buildings, machinery, mainframe computers, elevators, assembly line equipment.

3. Supplies and Business Services

Short-lasting products and services that facilitate developing or managing the finished product. Supplies include operating supplies and maintenance items. Business services include maintenance/repair services and business advisory services.

Examples
Supplies: Lubricants, paper, pencils, cleaning materials.
Services: Legal services, advertising, consulting, IT support.

Equipment Computers, forklifts Shorter life than installations

Product Levels: The Product Hierarchy

Product Hierarchy is a system of grouping products from broad needs to specific items. It has six levels that help marketers understand product positioning.
Fig 6.1 — Six Levels of Product Hierarchy
1
Need Family — The core need that underlies the existence of a product family (e.g., security)
2
Product Family — All product classes that can satisfy a core need (e.g., savings, income, loans)
3
Product Class — A group of products within the family recognized as having a certain functional coherence (e.g., financial instruments)
4
Product Line — A group of products within a class that are closely related (e.g., life insurance policies)
5
Product Type — A group of items within a product line that share one of several possible forms (e.g., term life insurance)
6
Item/SKU — A distinct unit within a brand or product line distinguishable by size, price, appearance (e.g., ₹50,000 term policy)
Example — Maruti Suzuki Cars
Need Family: Transportation
Product Family: Vehicles
Product Class: Automobiles
Product Line: Maruti Suzuki (all cars)
Product Type: Maruti Swift (specific model)
Item/SKU: Maruti Swift ZXI Petrol 2024
Topic 7

Product Systems and Mixes

A Product System is a group of diverse but related items that function in a compatible manner. A Product Mix (or Product Assortment) is the set of all product lines and items that a particular seller offers for sale.

Dimensions of Product Mix

1. Width (Breadth)

Number of different product lines the company carries.

2. Length

Total number of items in the product mix (sum of all items across all product lines).

3. Depth

Number of variants (sizes, flavors, models) offered within each product line.

4. Consistency

How closely related the various product lines are in end use, production requirements, distribution channels, or other aspects.

Example — Hindustan Unilever Limited (HUL)
Width: Soaps, detergents, shampoos, skin care, oral care, tea, coffee, packaged foods.
Length: Over 100 different products across all lines.
Depth: Lifebuoy soap comes in 5+ sizes and 3+ variants.
Consistency: High — all are consumer packaged goods.
Dimension Definition Example (HUL)
Width Number of product lines 10+ lines (soaps, detergents, etc.)
Length Total items in mix 100+ products
Depth Variants per line Lifebuoy: 15 variants
Consistency Relatedness of lines High — all FMCG products
Topic 8

Product Line Analysis & Product Line Length

Product Line Analysis is the process of evaluating each product in a line to determine its contribution to profits and strategic goals. Product Line Length refers to the number of items in a product line.

Product Line Analysis Methods

1. Sales and Profit Analysis

Examine each product's sales contribution and profit margin. Typically, 20% of products generate 80% of profits (Pareto Principle).

2. Market Profile Analysis

Compare each product against competitors' offerings in terms of features, quality, and price.

Product Line Length Decisions

Line Stretching

Adding products beyond the current range. Can be downward (adding low-end products), upward (adding premium products), or two-way.

Line Filling

Adding more items within the current price range to fill gaps and deter competitors.

Line Modernization

Updating existing products rather than adding new ones.

Line Pruning

Removing weak or unprofitable products to improve overall profitability.

Example — Titan Watches
Line Stretching (Upward): Added premium 'Edge' and 'Xylys' collections.
Line Stretching (Downward): Added 'Sonata' for budget segment.
Line Filling: Added 'Neo' and 'Raga' for middle segments.
Line Pruning: Discontinued underperforming older models.
Optimal Line Length

The ideal product line length balances two goals: offering enough variety to satisfy customers (avoiding stockouts) while not having so many items that costs increase and profits decrease (cannibalization).

The Customer Value Hierarchy

Customer Value Hierarchy (by Philip Kotler) identifies five levels of product value, from the core benefit to the potential product that includes all future augmentations.
Fig 9.1 — Five Levels of Customer Value Hierarchy
1Core Benefit
↓
2Basic Product
↓
3Expected Product
↓
4Augmented Product
↓
5Potential Product

Level 1: Core Benefit

The fundamental need or benefit the customer is really buying. For a hotel, the core benefit is "rest and sleep."

Level 2: Basic Product

The basic version of the product that delivers the core benefit. For a hotel: room, bed, bathroom, desk.

Level 3: Expected Product

The set of attributes and conditions buyers normally expect. For a hotel: clean sheets, working lights, towels, soap.

Level 4: Augmented Product

Additional services and benefits that exceed customer expectations. For a hotel: free Wi-Fi, swimming pool, gym, complimentary breakfast.

Level 5: Potential Product

All possible augmentations and transformations the product might undergo in the future. For a hotel: robot butlers, smart rooms, virtual reality experiences.

Example — Maruti Suzuki Car
Core Benefit: Transportation
Basic Product: Car with engine, wheels, seats, steering
Expected Product: Reliable engine, safety features, comfortable seats
Augmented Product: Free service for 3 years, roadside assistance, GPS navigation
Potential Product: Self-driving capability, flying cars
Exam Tip

Competition happens at the AUGMENTED PRODUCT level. Most companies can match the expected product, but winners go beyond expectations.

Topic 10

New Product Development

New Product Development (NPD) is the process of bringing a new product to market. It involves systematic steps from idea generation to commercialization.

Need for New Product Development

  • Changing Customer Needs: Customer preferences evolve over time.
  • Technological Advances: New technologies create new product opportunities.
  • Product Lifecycle: Existing products decline and need replacement.
  • Competitive Pressure: Competitors launch new products.
  • Growth and Profitability: New products drive revenue growth.
  • Risk Diversification: Spreading risk across multiple products.

Booz Allen & Hamilton Classification Scheme for New Products

Category Description Risk Level Example
New-to-the-world Innovations that create entirely new markets Very High First smartphone, First PC
New-to-the-firm Products new to the company but existing in market High Titan entering watch market
Additions to product lines New products added to existing lines Medium New flavor of existing chips
Improvements/revisions Better versions of existing products Medium iPhone 14 to iPhone 15
Repositionings Existing products targeted to new segments Low Nike shoes for women
Cost reductions New products that provide similar performance at lower cost Low Generic medicines

Step 1: Idea Generation

Systematic search for new product ideas from internal sources (R&D, employees) and external sources (customers, competitors, distributors, suppliers).

Step 2: Idea Screening

Filtering ideas to drop poor ones. Evaluate against company objectives, resources, and market potential.

Step 3: Concept Development & Testing

Developing detailed product concepts and testing them with target customers for feedback.

Step 4: Marketing Strategy Development

Designing initial marketing strategy including target market, positioning, sales goals, and budget.

Step 5: Business Analysis

Reviewing projected sales, costs, and profits to determine if the product meets company objectives.

Step 6: Product Development

Developing physical product prototype and testing its functional and psychological characteristics.

Step 7: Test Marketing

Introducing product in limited geographic areas to test consumer response and marketing program effectiveness.

Step 8: Commercialization

Full launch of product into the market with full production and marketing support.

Example — Titan's Edge Collection
Idea: Ultra-thin premium watches for style-conscious professionals.
Screening: Aligned with Titan's premium segment strategy.
Concept Test: Focus groups of corporate executives.
Strategy: Premium pricing, exclusive showrooms.
Analysis: Projected ROI of 25% in 2 years.
Development: 18 months of R&D for thin movement.
Test Marketing: Launched in Mumbai and Delhi first.
Commercialization: National launch with A-list celebrity endorsement.
Topic 11

Sustainable Practices in Product Design & Go-to-Market Strategy

Sustainable Product Design considers environmental, social, and economic impacts throughout the product lifecycle. Go-to-Market (GTM) Strategy is the plan for launching a product to reach target customers.

Sustainable Practices in Product Design

1. Design for Environment (DfE)

Designing products to minimize environmental impact throughout their lifecycle from raw material extraction to disposal.

2. Cradle-to-Cradle Design

Products designed so all materials can be recycled or composted at end of life, creating a circular economy rather than linear "take-make-dispose" model.

3. Design for Disassembly

Products designed to be easily taken apart for repair, recycling, or component reuse. This extends product life and reduces waste.

4. Material Reduction (Lightweighting)

Using less material while maintaining product quality and durability. Reduces resource consumption and shipping costs.

5. Use of Recycled Materials

Incorporating post-consumer recycled content into new products, reducing demand for virgin materials.

6. Energy Efficiency

Designing products that consume less energy during use, reducing carbon footprint and operating costs for customers.

7. Biodegradable Materials

Using materials that naturally decompose without harming the environment.

Example — Patagonia (Global)
Patagonia uses recycled polyester, organic cotton, and offers free repairs. Their "Don't Buy This Jacket" campaign encouraged customers to reduce consumption. Products are designed to last and be recyclable.
Example — Tata Motors (India)
Tata Motors focuses on electric vehicles, uses recycled materials in interiors, and has battery recycling programs. The Nexon EV is designed with sustainable practices including regenerative braking and eco-friendly interior materials.
Example — Paper Boat (India)
Paper Boat uses aseptic paper packaging (recyclable) instead of plastic. Their packaging is designed to be minimal and environmentally friendly.

Go-to-Market (GTM) Strategy

A Go-to-Market (GTM) Strategy is a detailed action plan that outlines how a company will launch a new product to reach target customers and achieve competitive advantage. It answers the question: "How will we get this product into customers' hands?"

Topic 12

Branding: Concept, Definition, Commodity vs Brand, Product vs Brand, Brand Equity

A Brand is a name, term, sign, symbol, design, or combination of these that identifies the maker or seller of a product and differentiates it from competitors. Branding is the process of creating a unique identity and emotional connection for a product in the customer's mind.

Definition of Brand

According to the American Marketing Association (AMA), "A brand is a name, term, design, symbol, or any other feature that identifies one seller's good or service as distinct from those of other sellers."

According to Philip Kotler, "A brand is a name, term, sign, symbol, or design, or a combination of them, intended to identify the goods or services of one seller or group of sellers and to differentiate them from those of competitors."

Example — Coca-Cola Brand
The Coca-Cola brand includes: the name "Coca-Cola," the red color, the unique bottle shape (contour bottle), the script logo, the dynamic ribbon, the polar bear mascot, and the emotional associations of happiness, refreshment, and togetherness.

Difference Explained — Parle vs Parle-G
Product: Glucose biscuits — many companies make them (Britannia, Priya Gold, Sunfeast). They are functionally similar.
Brand: Parle-G — associated with taste, value for money, trust, childhood memories, Indian identity, and emotional connection across generations. That's why people ask for "Parle-G" not just "glucose biscuits".
Difference Explained — Titan vs Generic Watch
Product: A watch tells time. A generic watch does that job at low cost.
Brand: Titan — associated with trust, gifting occasions, Indian craftsmanship, elegance, and aspirational value. People gift Titan watches for weddings, anniversaries, and achievements.

Concept of Brand Equity

Brand Equity is the added value endowed to products and services with a brand name. It reflects what consumers feel and think about the brand. A brand has positive brand equity when customers react more favorably to the brand than to an unbranded or generic version.

David Aaker's Brand Equity Model (5 Assets)

1. Brand Loyalty

Customers consistently choose this brand over others even when alternatives are available or cheaper.

Benefit: Reduced marketing costs, predictable revenue, competitive barrier.

Example: Apple users upgrade to new iPhones without considering other brands.

2. Brand Awareness

Customers recognize and recall the brand easily. Top-of-mind awareness means brand is the first that comes to mind in its category.

Benefit: Familiarity, trust, consideration set inclusion.

Example: "Xerox" for photocopying, "Google" for search, "Colgate" for toothpaste.

3. Perceived Quality

Customer perception of product quality — often exceeds actual objective quality. It's what customers believe, not necessarily reality.

Benefit: Premium pricing, differentiation, purchase justification.

Example: Mercedes = high quality, premium engineering, luxury.

4. Brand Associations

Unique images, attributes, benefits, and emotions linked to the brand in customers' minds.

Benefit: Differentiation, positioning, emotional connection.

Example: Volvo = safety; BMW = driving pleasure; Harley-Davidson = freedom and rebellion.

5. Other Proprietary Assets

Patents, trademarks, copyrights, exclusive distribution relationships, trade secrets that create competitive barriers.

Benefit: Legal protection, prevents copying, sustainable advantage.

Example: Coca-Cola's secret formula (trade secret), Google's search algorithm (patent).

Example — Tata Brand Equity in India (Strong Brand Equity)
Loyalty: Indian consumers trust Tata products across categories — from salt to cars to software. Multi-generational loyalty.
Awareness: Over 90% brand recall in India across urban and rural areas.
Perceived Quality: Associated with trust, ethics, reliability, nation-building, and quality at fair price.
Associations: "Tata" means trust, integrity, philanthropy, and "India's most respected business group."
Assets: Strong distribution network, multiple successful businesses (Tata Motors, Tata Consultancy Services, Tata Steel, Titan, Tata Salt).
Result: Tata commands premium prices across categories.
Example — Brand Equity in FMCG (Nirma vs Surf Excel)
Nirma: Low price, "value for money" positioning, mass market appeal. Brand equity built on affordability.
Surf Excel: Premium price, "daag achhe hain" (stains are good) positioning, emotional appeal. Brand equity built on superior cleaning, brand love, and family values.

Benefits of Strong Brand Equity

  • Premium Pricing: Customers pay more for the brand (e.g., Apple iPhones cost more than similar spec Android phones).
  • Customer Loyalty: Repeat purchases and resistance to switching.
  • Lower Marketing Costs: Less spending needed to create awareness.
  • Trade Cooperation: Retailers want to stock strong brands.
  • Brand Extensions: Easy to launch new products under the same brand (e.g., Tata from salt to cars).
  • Competitive Barrier: Difficult for competitors to copy brand equity.
  • Higher Margins: Premium pricing leads to better profitability.
Key Takeaway

A product is made in a factory; a brand is created in the customer's mind. Brands command premium prices, generate loyalty, and provide long-term competitive advantage. Building brand equity takes years of consistent delivery on promises, but it is the most valuable asset a company can own.

Packaging & Labeling

Packaging refers to the activities of designing and producing the container or wrapper for a product. It protects the product and communicates value. Labeling refers to the printed information appearing on or attached to the package, providing identification, description, and legal compliance.

Meaning & Role of Packaging

Packaging is not just a container — it is the "silent salesman" that attracts attention, communicates value, and influences purchase decisions at the point of sale.

Six Functions of Packaging

FunctionDescriptionExample
ProtectionProtects product from physical damage, spoilage, contamination, and tamperingBubble wrap, vacuum sealing, tamper-evident seals
ContainmentHolds the product together in a manageable formToothpaste in tube, chips in packet, milk in bottle
InformationCommunicates product details, usage instructions, ingredients, warningsNutrition facts panel, expiry date, MRP, manufacturing date
PromotionAttracts attention, communicates brand value, encourages purchaseColorful designs, celebrity images, promotional offers printed on pack
ConvenienceEasy to store, open, use, transport, and disposeEasy-open lids, resealable zippers, carry handles
DifferentiationDistinguishes from competitors on crowded retail shelvesUnique bottle shape (Coca-Cola contour bottle), distinctive colors (Tiffany blue)

Types of Packaging

TypeDescriptionExample
Primary Packaging Directly contains the product — touches the product itself. This is the last layer before the consumer uses the product. Biscuit wrapper, toothpaste tube, soft drink bottle, soap wrapper, chip packet.
Secondary Packaging Contains primary packaging. Used for grouping multiple units. Removed before product use. Cardboard box containing 10 toothpaste tubes, shrink wrap around 24 soft drink cans, display box for chocolates.
Tertiary Packaging Used for bulk handling, warehouse storage, and transportation. Not seen by end consumers. Corrugated shipping boxes, pallets with stretch wrap, shipping containers.
Example — Amul Butter Packaging
Primary: Aluminum foil wrapper directly around the butter.
Secondary: Cardboard carton box (often containing 10-20 butter packs).
Tertiary: Large corrugated box for transport to distributors and retailers.

Sustainable Practices in Packaging

With growing environmental awareness, sustainable packaging is becoming a competitive necessity. The "3R" principle has expanded to "7Rs":

  • Reduce: Using less packaging material (lightweighting) — e.g., thinner plastic bottles, smaller boxes.
  • Reuse: Designing packaging that can be refilled or reused multiple times — e.g., glass bottles (refillable), jute bags.
  • Recycle: Using recyclable materials like paper, glass, aluminum, and specific plastics (PET, HDPE).
  • Renewable: Using materials from renewable sources — e.g., bamboo, sugarcane bagasse, cornstarch-based bioplastics.
  • Recycled Content: Using post-consumer recycled (PCR) materials in new packaging — e.g., recycled PET bottles.
  • Refuse: Eliminating unnecessary packaging altogether — e.g., selling loose products, bulk bins.
  • Rot (Compost): Using compostable materials that biodegrade — e.g., plant-based plastics, paper.
Example — Sustainable Packaging in India
Amul: Uses minimal, recyclable packaging for milk and dairy products. Milk is often sold in reusable glass bottles in many cities.
Paper Boat Drinks: Uses aseptic paper packaging (recyclable, made from renewable resources) instead of plastic bottles.
Bisleri: Introduced "Bisleri Recycled" bottles made from 100% recycled PET. Also offers "Bisleri Connect" app for empty bottle collection and recycling.
Amazon India: Uses 100% recyclable packaging, eliminates single-use plastic, and introduced "Frustration-Free Packaging" (no excess boxes).
The Body Shop: Uses recycled plastic bottles, offers in-store recycling for empty containers, and has refill stations in select stores.
ITC (Fabelle chocolates): Uses FSC-certified paper boxes (from responsibly managed forests).
Example — IKEA Sustainable Packaging
IKEA has replaced Styrofoam with mushroom-based packaging (mycelium) that is compostable and biodegradable. They also use corrugated cardboard made from recycled materials and design packaging to be flat-packed (reducing shipping volume and emissions).

Labeling: Meaning & Role

A label is a tag, brand, or informative display attached to the product or its package. It serves as the product's identity card.

Six Functions of Labeling

  • Identification: Helps identify the product and brand name.
  • Description: Provides product specifications, ingredients, features, and instructions.
  • Promotion: Attracts customers, communicates benefits, and encourages purchase.
  • Legal Compliance: Mandatory information required by law (MRP, expiry date, manufacturing date, legal warnings).
  • Traceability: Batch numbers, lot numbers, serial numbers for quality control and recalls.
  • Authentication: Holograms, QR codes, scratch codes to verify product genuineness and prevent counterfeiting.

A Maggi noodles packet must display:

Sustainable Practices in Labeling

  • Minimal Labeling (Direct Printing): Printing directly on the package instead of using separate paper/plastic labels — reduces material waste.
  • Recyclable Label Materials: Using labels made from recycled paper or biodegradable materials (e.g., compostable adhesives).
  • Soy-based and Water-based Inks: Using eco-friendly inks instead of petroleum-based inks for printing labels.
  • Digital Labels (QR Codes): Replacing long printed information with small QR codes that customers scan for complete details — reduces ink and paper usage.
  • Reusable Labels: Labels designed to stay on reusable containers through multiple cycles (e.g., glass bottle labels that withstand washing).
  • Eco-certifications: Labels that communicate sustainability credentials (e.g., "Made from recycled material," "Plastic Neutral," "Carbon Neutral," "Eco-friendly packaging").
  • Removable Labels: Labels that can be easily removed to enable container reuse or recycling.
Example — QR Code for Sustainable Labeling
Many brands now print a small QR code on the package instead of long instruction manuals or detailed product information. Customers scan the QR code to access:
• Complete product specifications
• Instruction manual (saving paper)
• Video tutorials
• Recycling instructions
• Warranty registration
This reduces paper, ink, and packaging waste. Examples: Samsung electronics, Xiaomi products, and many FMCG brands.
Example — Sustainable Labeling in India
Bamboo India: Uses minimal labeling with soy-based inks on recycled paper.
Bare Necessities (India): Uses compostable labels and zero-plastic packaging.
Phool (India): Uses handmade recycled paper labels printed with vegetable dyes.
Amrut (Indian single malt): Uses minimalist labeling with recycled paper and eco-friendly inks.
Key Takeaway

Packaging and labeling are the "silent salesmen" of the product. They protect the product, communicate value, and influence purchase decisions at the point of sale. Sustainable packaging and labeling are becoming competitive necessities as environmental awareness grows among Indian consumers. Companies that ignore sustainability risk losing customers to eco-conscious competitors.

📌 Important Points to Remember

• Product is anything that satisfies customer needs — includes goods, services, experiences, and ideas.

Case Study 1 — Amul (Domestic - Goods)
Product: Amul Butter, Cheese, Ice Cream, Milk.
Traditional Practice: "Utterly Butterly Delicious" mascot (Amul Girl) — print ads, billboards, hoardings since 1966. Cooperative model connecting farmers directly.
Contemporary Practice: Social media engagement (Amul memes on current events), online store (Amul Shop), WhatsApp ordering, digital campaigns, rapid response marketing (same-day topical ads).
Key Learning: Amul has maintained brand relevance for 50+ years by adapting from traditional outdoor advertising to real-time social media marketing without changing core brand identity.
Case Study 2 — Paper Boat (Domestic - E-Product Contemporary)
Product: Traditional Indian beverages (Aamras, Jaljeera, Anar, Aampanna) in modern packaging.
Traditional Practice: Not applicable (created new category).
Contemporary Practice: Nostalgia-driven marketing ("Drinks and Memories"), storytelling packaging (poems on packs), Instagram-first campaigns, QR codes to access recipes and stories, limited edition flavors, D2C website, experiential pop-ups.
Key Learning: Created a new market by combining traditional recipes with contemporary packaging and digital-first storytelling.
Case Study 3 — Apple iPhone (International - E-Product/Goods)
Product: iPhone (smartphone).
Traditional Practice: TV commercials (iconic "1984" ad), print ads, keynote presentations (Steve Jobs era), retail stores (Apple Store experience).
Contemporary Practice: Live-streamed product launch events (now virtual), social media teasers, influencer unboxings, direct-to-consumer via Apple.com, Apple Trade In program, AppleCare+ subscription, App Store ecosystem lock-in.
Key Learning: Moved from mass advertising to community building, ecosystem lock-in, and direct customer relationships.
